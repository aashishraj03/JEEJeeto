Electric Charge

Definition

Electric charge (\(q\) or \(Q\)) is a special property of matter that produces and experiences electric and magnetic effects. Because of it, particles attract or repel each other by an electric force, which is different from the gravitational force.

In simple words: charge is to electric force what mass is to gravity. Gravity only pulls, but electric force can also push.

Note

Repulsion is the sure test of electrification. Attraction alone is not proof, because a charged body also attracts a neutral body.

Types of charge

Positive chargeNegative charge
The body has a deficiency of electrons compared with protons.The body has an excess of electrons compared with protons.

Simple view: only electrons move from body to body. Losing electrons makes a body positive; gaining electrons makes it negative.

Unit and dimension

  • Charge is a scalar quantity.
  • SI unit: coulomb (C). \(1\,\text{C} = 1\,\text{A·s}\), so \(q = it\).
  • CGS unit: electrostatic unit (esu), with \(1\,\text{C} = 3\times10^{9}\,\text{esu}\).
  • Dimension: \([q] = [\mathrm{A\,T}]\).

Fundamental charge and quantisation

The minimum charge that can exist freely in nature is the fundamental charge \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}\). An electron carries \(-e\) and a proton carries \(+e\).

Quantisation of charge \[ q = \pm\, n e \]

\(n\) is an integer: the number of electrons in excess (or deficiency) compared with the neutral state.

Simple view: charge comes in whole packets of \(e\), like money that cannot be split below one coin.

Quarks (inside the proton, neutron and other particles) carry \(\pm e/3\) and \(\pm 2e/3\). A proton has \(\tfrac{2e}{3}+\tfrac{2e}{3}-\tfrac{e}{3}=+e\). Free charge is always an integral multiple of \(e\).

Properties of charge

  • Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
  • Conservation. Charge can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred from one body to another. For \(A + B \to C + D\): \(q_1 + q_2 = q_3 + q_4\).
  • Charge cannot be converted into energy (unlike mass). Charge never exists without mass, but mass can exist without charge.
  • Charge is independent of the speed of the body. Mass increases with speed as \(m = \dfrac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}\) while charge stays the same, so the specific charge \(e/m\) decreases as speed increases.
  • A stationary charge produces only an electric field.
  • A charge moving with constant velocity (zero acceleration) produces an electric field and also a magnetic field around it.
  • A charge moving with variable velocity produces electric and magnetic fields and also radiates energy as an electromagnetic wave.

Solved examples

Example 1 · Specific charge of a speeding particle

A charged particle is at rest. It starts moving with increasing speed. What happens to the magnitude of its specific charge \(e/m\)?

Charge does not depend on speed, so \(e\) stays constant. The mass depends on speed:

\[ m = \frac{m_{rest}}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} \]

As \(v\) increases, \(m\) increases.

Answer: \(e/m\) decreases.

Example 2 · Electrons in 18 g of water

Find the total number of electrons in 18 g of water.

The molar mass of water is 18 g, so 18 g is 1 mole, i.e. \(N_A\) molecules. Each H₂O molecule has \(8 + 2 = 10\) electrons.

\[ N_e = 10\,N_A \approx 6.02\times10^{24} \]

Answer: \(10N_A\) electrons (\(\approx 6\times10^{24}\)). Their total charge magnitude is \(10N_Ae \approx 9.6\times10^{5}\) C.

Simple view: in a conductor the nuclei stay fixed while the outer (free) electrons wander through the material. A conductor placed near a charge shows induced charges \(+q_i\) and \(-q_i\) of equal magnitude.

Free electrons in a metal are loosely bound to their atoms, while the nuclei remain fixed.

Methods of Charging a Body

By friction

Two neutral bodies (at least one an insulator) are rubbed together and then separated: they become oppositely charged. The body with the greater electron affinity becomes negative. Both get equal magnitudes: \(|-q| = |+q|\).

By conduction

A charged conductor is connected to a neutral conductor. Charge flows until the potentials of the two connected bodies become the same. By conservation of charge, \(Q_0 = q_1 + q_2\).

Identical bodies \[ q_1' = q_2' = \frac{q_1 + q_2}{2} \]

A charged body with \(Q_0\) touching an identical neutral body leaves \(Q_0/2\) on each.

By induction

A charged body is brought near (without touching) a neutral conductor. The conductor is then earthed through a switch, so electrons flow between the conductor and the earth. After the earth connection is removed and the charged body taken away, the conductor is left with a charge opposite to that of the inducing body. A positive inducing charge makes the conductor negative.

  • In any conducting material, charge always resides on the surface, not inside the body.
  • A point charge near a neutral conductor is always attracted: the induced opposite charge is nearer, so the attractive force \(F_1\) is larger than the repulsive force \(F_2\).
  • Near a charged conductor, attraction also takes place when the attractive force exceeds the repulsion, i.e. \(F_1 > F_2 + F_3\).

Solved examples

Example 1 · A point charge near a neutral and a charged conductor

A point charge \(+q\) is placed near (i) a neutral conductor and (ii) a charged conductor. Compare the forces and say whether attraction can take place.

(i) Neutral conductor. The induced negative charge is nearer to \(+q\), the induced positive charge is farther. Since force falls with distance, the attraction exceeds the repulsion: \(F_1 > F_2\). Net force: attraction.

(ii) Charged conductor. Besides the induced charges, the conductor's own like charge repels \(+q\). If \(F_1 > F_2 + F_3\), the net force is attraction.

Answer: a neutral conductor is always attracted; a charged one can also be attracted when \(F_1 > F_2 + F_3\).

Coulomb's Law

Statement

The magnitude of the electric force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

In vacuum

\(F_{el} \propto q_1 q_2\) and \(F_{el} \propto \dfrac{1}{r^2}\).

Formula \[ F_{el} = \frac{K q_1 q_2}{r^2}, \qquad K = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \]
SystemValue of \(K\)
SI\(9\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
CGS\(1\ \text{dyne cm}^2/\text{esu}^2\)

\(\varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\ \text{C}^2\,\text{N}^{-1}\text{m}^{-2}\) is the permittivity of free space (vacuum).

In a material medium

Two point charges \(q_1, q_2\) are placed in a medium at separation \(r\).

Formula \[ (F_{el})_{medium} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon}\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_r}(F_{el})_{vacuum} \] \[ \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\,\varepsilon_r, \qquad \varepsilon_r = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0} \]
  • \(\varepsilon\) is the absolute permittivity of the medium; \(\varepsilon_r\) is the relative permittivity (dielectric constant).
  • \(1 \le \varepsilon_r < \infty\). \(\varepsilon_r = 1\) for vacuum or air, and \(\varepsilon_r \to \infty\) for a conducting medium.
  • A medium simply reduces the force by the factor \(\varepsilon_r\).

Important points

  • The law applies directly to point charges only. For extended charge distributions, calculate the force by integration.
  • Electric force is a central force: it always acts along the line joining the two charges.
  • Electrostatic force is a conservative force.
  • The line of action of the force on \(q_2\) passes through \(q_1\), so the torque on \(q_2\) about \(q_1\) is zero. Hence the angular momentum of \(q_2\) about \(q_1\) is conserved.

Vector form

Take two point charges \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) with their signs, at position vectors \(\vec r_1\) and \(\vec r_2\). \(\vec F_{21}\) is the force on \(q_2\) due to \(q_1\).

Formula \[ \vec F_{21} = \frac{K q_1 q_2}{|\vec r_2 - \vec r_1|^2}\cdot\frac{(\vec r_2 - \vec r_1)}{|\vec r_2 - \vec r_1|} = \frac{K q_1 q_2}{|\vec r_2 - \vec r_1|^3}(\vec r_2 - \vec r_1) \]

Simplified form: if \(\vec r\) is the vector from the source charge \(q_1\) to the point where the force is wanted,

Formula \[ \vec F = \frac{K q_1 q_2}{r^3}\,\vec r = \frac{K q_1 q_2}{r^2}\,\hat r \]

Charges are put in with their signs, so a positive result means repulsion (along \(\hat r\)) and a negative result means attraction.

Standard results

Key results
  • Third charge in equilibrium.For charges \(q\) and \(4q\) a distance \(d\) apart, a third charge is in equilibrium at \(d/3\) from \(q\). For the whole system to be in equilibrium it must be \(-\dfrac{4q}{9}\). (Example 5, 6)
  • Square with a fifth charge.Four charges \(q\) at the corners of a square are in equilibrium with \(Q = -\dfrac q4\left(1+2\sqrt2\right)\) at the centre. (Example 7)
  • Cube.Eight equal charges \(q\) at the corners of a cube of side \(a\): force on one is \(\sqrt3\,\dfrac{Kq^2}{a^2}\left(1+\dfrac1{\sqrt2}+\dfrac1{3\sqrt3}\right)\) along the diagonal. (Example 2)
  • Regular polygon.Equal like charges at all vertices exert zero net force on a charge at the centre. (Example 10)
  • Circular motion.Around a fixed charge: \(v=\sqrt{\dfrac{Kqq_0}{mr}}\), \(f=\dfrac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\dfrac{Kqq_0}{mr^3}}\). (Example 3)
  • Hanging balls.\(\tan\theta=\dfrac{F_{el}}{mg}\), \(F_{el}=\dfrac{Kq^2}{4L^2\sin^2\theta}\); equal masses give equal angles; \(\theta\propto\dfrac1{\text{mass}}\); in a liquid \(\varepsilon_r=\dfrac{\rho_s}{\rho_s-\rho_l}\). (Examples 1)

Solved examples

Example 1 · Pendulums in air and in a liquid

Two identical simple pendulums hang from a common point. The bobs have mass \(m\) and charge \(q\) each, and in equilibrium the angle between the strings is \(\theta\) (taken in the PDF as the angle made by each string with the vertical). The whole set-up is placed in a liquid and the angle stays \(\theta\). If the density of the bob material is \(\rho_s\) and of the liquid \(\rho_l\), find the relative permittivity (dielectric constant) of the liquid.

In air (balance of forces on a bob): \(T\cos\theta = mg\), \(T\sin\theta = F_{el}\), so

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{F_{el}}{mg} \]

In liquid: electric force becomes \(F_{el}/\varepsilon_r\) and buoyancy \(F_{up}=\rho_l V g\) acts upward, where \(m=\rho_s V\), so \(F_{up}=mg\,\dfrac{\rho_l}{\rho_s}=\dfrac{mg}{RD}\) with \(RD=\dfrac{\rho_s}{\rho_l}\):

\[ \tan\theta = \frac{F_{el}/\varepsilon_r}{mg - F_{up}} = \frac{F_{el}/\varepsilon_r}{mg\left(1-\frac1{RD}\right)} \]

The angle is unchanged, so equate the two:

\[ \frac{F_{el}}{mg} = \frac{F_{el}}{\varepsilon_r\,mg\left(1-\frac1{RD}\right)} \]

Answer: \(\varepsilon_r = \dfrac{1}{1-\frac1{RD}} = \dfrac{\rho_s}{\rho_s-\rho_l}\).

Example 2 · Force on one of eight charges at the corners of a cube

Eight charges \(q\) (all identical) are placed at the eight corners of a cube of side \(a\). Find the net force acting on any one of them due to the remaining seven.

Take the charge at the origin. Group the other seven by distance (all forces on it are repulsive, directed away from each source):

  • 3 charges at distance \(a\) (along the edges): each gives \(\dfrac{Kq^2}{a^2}\); their vector sum is \(\dfrac{Kq^2}{a^2}(\hat i+\hat j+\hat k)\) (magnitude along the diagonal, directed away from the cube).
  • 3 charges at \(a\sqrt2\) (face diagonals): each gives \(\dfrac{Kq^2}{2a^2}\). Summing the three components gives \(\dfrac{Kq^2}{a^2}\cdot\dfrac1{\sqrt2}(\hat i+\hat j+\hat k)\).
  • 1 charge at \(a\sqrt3\) (body diagonal): \(\dfrac{Kq^2}{3a^2}\) along \(\dfrac{(\hat i+\hat j+\hat k)}{\sqrt3}\), i.e. \(\dfrac{Kq^2}{3\sqrt3\,a^2}(\hat i+\hat j+\hat k)\).
\[ \vec F = \frac{Kq^2}{a^2}\left(1+\frac1{\sqrt2}+\frac1{3\sqrt3}\right)(\hat i+\hat j+\hat k) \]

Answer: \(|\vec F| = \sqrt3\,\dfrac{Kq^2}{a^2}\left(1+\dfrac1{\sqrt2}+\dfrac1{3\sqrt3}\right)\), directed along the body diagonal away from the cube.

Example 3 · Circular motion around a fixed charge

A charged particle of mass \(m\) and charge \(q\) performs circular motion around a fixed positive charge \(+q_0\). If the radius of the circle is \(r\), find the speed and frequency of rotation.

\[ F_{el}=F_c \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{Kqq_0}{r^2}=\frac{mv^2}{r} \;\Rightarrow\; v=\sqrt{\frac{Kqq_0}{mr}} \] \[ \omega=\frac vr=\sqrt{\frac{Kqq_0}{mr^3}}, \qquad f=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{Kqq_0}{mr^3}} \]

Answer: \(v=\sqrt{Kqq_0/mr}\), \(\ f=\dfrac1{2\pi}\sqrt{Kqq_0/(mr^3)}\).

Example 4 · Motion between two fixed charges

Two charges \(+q\) are fixed at \((0,a)\) and \((0,-a)\). A particle of mass \(m\) and charge \(-q_0\) is at \((x,0)\). Find (i) the net electric force on it; (ii) if released from rest, the type of motion, the condition for SHM and the time period.

(i) Each force has magnitude \(F_0=\dfrac{Kqq_0}{a^2+x^2}\) (attraction towards the fixed charge). The components along \(y\) cancel; those along \(x\) (towards the origin) add:

\[ F_{net}=2F_0\cos\theta = 2\cdot\frac{Kqq_0}{a^2+x^2}\cdot\frac{x}{\sqrt{a^2+x^2}} = \frac{2Kqq_0\,x}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}} \]

(ii) The force always points towards the origin, so on release the particle performs oscillatory motion about the origin. It is not SHM in general because \(F\) is not proportional to \(x\). For SHM, \(x \ll a\), then \((a^2+x^2)^{3/2}\approx a^3\):

\[ F\approx \frac{2Kqq_0}{a^3}\,x \;(=kx),\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac mk}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{ma^3}{2Kqq_0}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,m\,a^3}{qq_0}} \]

Answer: \(F_{net}=\dfrac{2Kqq_0x}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\); oscillatory, SHM only for \(x\ll a\), with \(T=2\pi\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon_0ma^3/(qq_0)}\).

Example 5 · Third charge in equilibrium (charges q and 4q)

Two charges \(q\) and \(4q\) are separated by a distance \(d\). Where should a third charge be placed so that it can remain in equilibrium?

For equilibrium of the third charge (its sign and magnitude do not matter) the two forces must be equal and opposite, so it lies between the charges:

\[ F_q=F_{4q}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{Kqq_0}{x^2}=\frac{K\,4q\,q_0}{(d-x)^2}\;\Rightarrow\;(d-x)^2=4x^2\;\Rightarrow\;d-x=2x \]

Answer: \(x=\dfrac d3\) from the charge \(q\).

Example 6 · Third charge so that the whole system is in equilibrium

Charges \(q\) (at A) and \(4q\) (at B) are a distance \(d\) apart. Where must a third charge be placed so that the entire system is in equilibrium, and what is its value?

On C: \(F_A=F_B\) gives (as in Example 5) \(x=\dfrac d3\).

On A: the force from \(Q\) must cancel the force from \(4q\) (which repels A, away from B). So \(Q\) must attract A, so its sign is opposite to \(q\):

\[ \frac{KQq}{(d/3)^2}=\frac{K\,4q\cdot q}{d^2}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{9Q}{d^2}=\frac{4q}{d^2}\;\Rightarrow\;|Q|=\frac{4q}{9} \]

Answer: place it at \(d/3\) from \(q\) (between the charges); \(Q=-\dfrac{4q}{9}\).

Example 7 · Fifth charge for equilibrium of four charges at a square

Four identical charges \(q\) are at the corners A, B, C, D of a square of side \(a\). Where should a fifth charge \(Q\) be placed, and what should its value be, for the entire system to remain in equilibrium?

By symmetry, \(Q\) goes at the centre. Take corner A. Let \(F_0=\dfrac{Kq^2}{a^2}\).

  • From B and D (adjacent): \(F_0\) each, at right angles → resultant \(F_0\sqrt2\) along the diagonal, away from the centre.
  • From C (opposite, distance \(a\sqrt2\)): \(\dfrac{Kq^2}{2a^2}=\dfrac{F_0}{2}\), also away from the centre.
\[ F'=\frac{F_0}2+F_0\sqrt2 \]

The charge \(Q\) (opposite in sign to \(q\), distance \(a/\sqrt2\)) must provide an equal inward force:

\[ \frac{KqQ}{(a/\sqrt2)^2}=\frac{2KqQ}{a^2}=\frac{Kq^2}{a^2}\left(\frac12+\sqrt2\right)\;\Rightarrow\;Q=\frac q2\left(\frac12+\sqrt2\right)=\frac q4\left(1+2\sqrt2\right) \]

Answer: at the centre of the square, \(Q=-\dfrac q4\left(1+2\sqrt2\right)\).

Example 8 · A square of charges in the xz plane

A square with its centre at the origin and side \(a\) lies in the xz plane. Four identical charges \(q\) are fixed at its four corners. Find the net electric force on a charge \(Q\) placed at \((0,y,0)\), and discuss the motion.

Each corner is at distance \(r=\sqrt{y^2+a^2/2}\) from \(Q\), so each force has magnitude \(F_0=\dfrac{KqQ}{r^2}\). The four sideways components cancel in pairs; the components along the y-axis add, each with \(\cos\varphi=\dfrac yr\):

\[ F_{net}=4F_0\cos\varphi=\frac{4KqQ\,y}{\left(y^2+\frac{a^2}2\right)^{3/2}}\quad\text{(along the y-axis)} \]

If \(Q\) has the sign opposite to \(q\), this force points towards the origin, so \(Q\) executes oscillatory motion about the centre.

Answer: \(F_{net}=\dfrac{4KqQy}{(y^2+a^2/2)^{3/2}}\) along the axis; oscillation when \(Q\) and \(q\) are of opposite sign.

Example 9 · Charges at the vertices of a regular pentagon

Charges \(q\) are at the five vertices of a regular pentagon A, B, C, D, E and a charge \(Q\) is at its centre (vertex distance \(r\)). Find the net force on \(Q\) if (i) the charge at C is removed; (ii) the charges at C and D are removed; (iii) the charge at C is replaced by \(-q\); (iv) the charge at C is replaced by \(+2q\).

With all five charges equal, the forces on \(Q\) cancel: \(\vec F_A+\vec F_B+\vec F_C+\vec F_D+\vec F_E=0\). Let \(F=\dfrac{KqQ}{r^2}\) be the magnitude of any one force.

  • (i) C removed: the other four forces sum to \(-\vec F_C\). Net force \(=\dfrac{KqQ}{r^2}\), directed as if due to the missing charge reversed.
  • (ii) C and D removed: the net force is the sum of the two removed forces, reversed. They make \(72^\circ\) with each other, so \(F_{net}=2F\cos36^\circ=\dfrac{2KqQ}{r^2}\cos36^\circ\), along the bisector of angle DQC.
  • (iii) C replaced by \(-q\): the force of C reverses: \(-\vec F_C\) (others) \(+(-\vec F_C)\) \(=-2\vec F_C\). Net \(=\dfrac{2KqQ}{r^2}\).
  • (iv) C replaced by \(+2q\): \(-\vec F_C+2\vec F_C=\vec F_C\). Net \(=\dfrac{KqQ}{r^2}\).

Answer: (i) \(KqQ/r^2\); (ii) \(\dfrac{2KqQ}{r^2}\cos36^\circ\); (iii) \(2KqQ/r^2\); (iv) \(KqQ/r^2\).

Example 10 · Charge at the centre of a regular polygon

Show that the net force on a charge placed at the centre of any regular polygon is zero if charges of equal magnitude and same sign are placed at its vertices.

Every vertex is at the same distance from the centre, so every force has the same magnitude. The forces are spaced at equal angles round the centre, so they form a closed polygon and their vector sum is zero.

Answer: \(\vec F_{net}=0\) (also holds when removing or changing charges by superposition, as in Example 9).

Important results
  • \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{F_{el}}{mg}\) with \(F_{el}=\dfrac{Kq^2}{4L^2\sin^2\theta}\) (strings of length \(L\), separation \(2L\sin\theta\)).
  • Equal masses with charges \(q_1,q_2\): \(\tan\theta_1=\tan\theta_2=\dfrac{F_{el}}{mg}\), so \(\theta_1=\theta_2\). The angle depends only on the masses (not on the charge values).
  • Masses \(m_1,m_2\): \(\tan\theta_1=\dfrac{F_{el}}{m_1g}\), \(\tan\theta_2=\dfrac{F_{el}}{m_2g}\), so \(\theta\propto\dfrac1{\text{mass}}\); with \(2m\) and \(4m\): \(\tan\theta_1=\dfrac{F_{el}}{2mg}\), \(\tan\theta_2=\dfrac{F_{el}}{4mg}\), \(\theta_1>\theta_2\).

Electric Field

The electric field \(\vec E\) at a point is the force per unit positive test charge, so a charge \(q\) placed there feels \(\vec F = q\vec E\) (see force on a charge in an external field). Fields of several charges add as vectors.

Point charge

Formula \[ E = \frac{Kq}{r^2} \]
  • For a positive point charge the field is directed away from the charge (radially outward).
  • For a negative point charge the field is directed towards the charge (radially inward).

Solved examples (point charges)

Example 1 · Field at two points on the x-axis

Charges \(+q\) at \((-a,0)\) and \(-q\) at \((a,0)\). Find the electric field at (i) the origin, (ii) \((2a,0)\).

(i) At the origin (distance \(a\) from each). The field of \(+q\) points away from it (+x); the field of \(-q\) points toward it (also +x):

\[ E_O=\frac{Kq}{a^2}+\frac{Kq}{a^2}=\frac{2Kq}{a^2}\quad(+x) \]

(ii) At \((2a,0)\): distance \(3a\) from \(+q\) (field +x) and \(a\) from \(-q\) (field towards \(-q\), i.e. \(-x\)):

\[ E=\frac{Kq}{9a^2}-\frac{Kq}{a^2}=-\frac{8Kq}{9a^2} \]

Answer: (i) \(\dfrac{2Kq}{a^2}\) along \(+x\); (ii) \(\dfrac{8Kq}{9a^2}\) along \(-x\).

Example 2 · Graph of E against x and against y (charges +q and −q)

For \(+q\) at \((-a,0)\) and \(-q\) at \((a,0)\), draw the graph of the electric field (i) versus \(x\) and (ii) versus \(y\). Convention: take the field with a positive sign along the +x axis and a negative sign along the −x axis.

E versus x. Between the charges both fields point along +x, so \(E>0\); it is minimum (\(\dfrac{2Kq}{a^2}\)) at the middle and rises without limit near either charge. Outside the charges the nearer charge dominates and the field points along \(-x\): \(E<0\), tending to zero as \(|x|\to\infty\).

E versus y. On the perpendicular bisector the components along y cancel, leaving \(E_x>0\) with maximum \(\dfrac{2Kq}{a^2}\) at \(y=0\), decreasing symmetrically to zero as \(|y|\to\infty\).

Answer: curves as in the figure.

Example 3 · Graph of E against x for two equal positive charges

Draw the \(E\)–\(x\) graph for \(+q\) at \((-a,0)\) and \(+q\) at \((a,0)\) (field positive along +x).

At the midpoint the fields cancel, \(E=0\). For \(-a0\)); for \(0a\) the field points along +x (positive, falling to zero); for \(x<-a\) it points along \(-x\) (negative, rising to zero).

Answer: an odd curve passing through the origin, with the signs described above.

Electric field due to a uniform line charge

A line (rod) with uniform linear density \(\lambda\). We find the field at a point \(P\) at perpendicular distance \(d\) from the line in terms of \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(\lambda\) and \(d\). \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are the angles that the lines from \(P\) to the two ends make with the perpendicular from \(P\).

Derivation

An element \(dy\) carries \(dq = \lambda\,dy\) and gives \(dE = \dfrac{K\,dq}{r^2}\). Let \(\theta\) be the angle of the element's line from \(P\) with the perpendicular. Then \(y = d\tan\theta\), \(dy = d\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\), \(r^2 = d^2\sec^2\theta\). Components:

\[ dE_\perp = dE\cos\theta = \frac{K\lambda}{d}\cos\theta\,d\theta, \qquad dE_\parallel = dE\sin\theta = \frac{K\lambda}{d}\sin\theta\,d\theta \]

Integrate from \(\theta = -\beta\) to \(\theta = +\alpha\):

\[ E_\perp = \frac{K\lambda}{d}\left[\sin\alpha + \sin\beta\right], \qquad E_\parallel = \frac{K\lambda}{d}\left[\cos\beta - \cos\alpha\right] \]
Result \[ E_{net} = \sqrt{E_\perp^2 + E_\parallel^2}, \qquad \tan\phi = \frac{E_\parallel}{E_\perp} \]

\(\phi\) is the angle of the net field from the perpendicular direction.

Special cases of a line charge

Key results
  • Rod on both sides of the perpendicular: \(E_\perp=\dfrac{K\lambda}{d}[\sin\alpha+\sin\beta]\), \(E_\parallel=\dfrac{K\lambda}{d}[\cos\beta-\cos\alpha]\).
  • Point beyond one end (both ends on the same side of the perpendicular): \(E_\perp=\dfrac{K\lambda}{d}[\sin\alpha-\sin\beta]\), \(E_\parallel=\dfrac{K\lambda}{d}[\cos\beta-\cos\alpha]\).
  • Symmetric point (\(\alpha=\beta\)): \(E_\perp=\dfrac{2K\lambda\sin\alpha}{d}\), \(E_\parallel=0\).
  • Infinite wire (\(\alpha=\beta=90^\circ\)): \(E_\parallel=0\), \(E=\dfrac{2K\lambda}{d}\), perpendicular to the wire (away for \(+\lambda\), towards for \(-\lambda\)); in vector form \(\vec E=\dfrac{2K\lambda}{d}\hat n\).
  • Semi-infinite wire, point on the perpendicular through the end (\(\alpha=90^\circ,\beta=0\)): \(E_\perp=E_\parallel=\dfrac{K\lambda}{d}\), \(E_{net}=\dfrac{\sqrt2K\lambda}{d}\) at \(45^\circ\).

Field due to a charged arc at its centre

A circular arc of radius \(R\) and total charge \(Q\) subtends angle \(\theta\) at the centre. Then \(\lambda = \dfrac{Q}{R\theta}\) and \(dq = \lambda R\,d\alpha\), where \(\alpha\) is measured from the bisector.

Derivation

By symmetry about the bisector, the components \(dE\sin\alpha\) cancel. Only \(dE\cos\alpha\) survives:

\[ E = \int_{-\theta/2}^{\theta/2}\frac{K\lambda R\,d\alpha}{R^2}\cos\alpha = \frac{2K\lambda}{R}\sin\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{KQ}{R^2}\cdot\frac{\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta/2} \]
Formula \[ E = \frac{2K\lambda}{R}\sin\frac{\theta}{2} \]

The field is directed along the angle bisector. If \(\theta \ll 1\), \(E \approx \dfrac{KQ}{R^2}\) (a point charge).

Field on the axis of a uniformly charged ring

A ring of radius \(R\) carries charge \(Q\) uniformly (\(\lambda=\dfrac Q{2\pi R}\), \(dq=\lambda\,dL\)). We find the field on the axis at distance \(x\) from the centre.

Derivation

Each element gives \(dE = \dfrac{K\,dq}{R^2 + x^2}\). The components perpendicular to the axis cancel by symmetry; only \(dE\cos\theta\) with \(\cos\theta = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}}\) survives:

\[ E = \int dE\cos\theta = \frac{Kx}{(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\int dq = \frac{KQx}{(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} \]
Formula \[ E = \frac{KQx}{(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} \]
  • The field is zero at the centre (\(x = 0\)) and as \(x \to \infty\).
  • If \(x \gg R\): \(E \approx \dfrac{KQ}{x^2}\) (acts like a point charge).
  • Maximum field. From \(\dfrac{dE}{dx}=KQ\,\dfrac{(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}-3x^2(R^2+x^2)^{1/2}}{(R^2+x^2)^3}=0\): \(R^2+x^2-3x^2=0\), so \(R^2=2x^2\), \(x=\pm\dfrac R{\sqrt2}\). Substituting, \(E_{max}=\dfrac{2KQ}{3\sqrt3\,R^2}\).

Field on the axis of a uniformly charged disc

A disc of radius \(R\) with uniform surface density \(\sigma = \dfrac{Q}{\pi R^2}\). The disc is a combination of infinitely many concentric rings with radii from \(0\) to \(R\). A ring of radius \(r\) and width \(dr\) has area \(2\pi r\,dr\) and charge \(dQ=\sigma\,2\pi r\,dr\).

Derivation \[ E = \int_0^R \frac{K\sigma\,2\pi r\,dr\;x}{(r^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\sigma x}{2\varepsilon_0}\int_0^R \frac{r\,dr}{(r^2+x^2)^{3/2}} \]

Put \(r^2 + x^2 = t\), \(r\,dr = dt/2\):

\[ E = \frac{\sigma x}{2\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + R^2}}\right] \]
Formula \[ E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left[1 - \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + R^2}}\right] = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left[1 - \cos\theta\right] \]

\(\theta\) is the semi-conical angle of the cone with apex at the point and the disc as base.

Field due to an infinitely large uniformly charged sheet

For an infinite sheet \(\theta \to 90^\circ\), so \(\cos\theta \to 0\):

Formula \[ E_{sheet} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} \]

This is a constant quantity: it is not a function of \(x\) (the distance from the sheet).

When two parallel layers each of density \(\sigma\) are combined (a thin sheet with \(\sigma\) on each face), each contributes \(\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\) and the net field outside is \(\dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\). The same result follows from Gauss's law.

Solved examples (line, arc, ring)

Example 1 · Finite wire seen at 53° and 37°

A uniformly charged wire (density \(\lambda\)) subtends angles \(\alpha=53^\circ\) and \(\beta=37^\circ\) at a point \(P\) at perpendicular distance \(d\). Find the net field.

\[ E_\perp=\frac{K\lambda}{d}(\sin53^\circ+\sin37^\circ)=\frac{K\lambda}{d}\left(\frac45+\frac35\right)=\frac{7K\lambda}{5d} \] \[ E_\parallel=\frac{K\lambda}{d}(\cos37^\circ-\cos53^\circ)=\frac{K\lambda}{d}\left(\frac45-\frac35\right)=\frac{K\lambda}{5d} \] \[ E_{net}=\sqrt{E_\perp^2+E_\parallel^2}=\frac{K\lambda}{d}\sqrt{\frac{49+1}{25}}=\frac{\sqrt2\,K\lambda}{d},\qquad \tan\phi=\frac{E_\parallel}{E_\perp}=\frac17 \]

Answer: \(E_{net}=\sqrt2K\lambda/d\), at \(\phi=\tan^{-1}(1/7)\) from the perpendicular.

Example 2 · Equilateral triangle of line charges, field at the centroid

Three thin rods of length \(a\) form an equilateral triangle: two carry \(+\lambda\) and one carries \(-\lambda\) (uniformly). Find the electric field at the centre. (Sign pattern read from the figure.)

The centre is at distance \(d=\dfrac{a}{2\sqrt3}\) from each side, and each rod subtends \(\alpha=\beta=60^\circ\) (symmetric): \(\sin\alpha=\dfrac{a/2}{\sqrt{a^2/4+a^2/12}}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\). The field of one rod:

\[ E_0=\frac{2K\lambda\sin60^\circ}{d}=2K\lambda\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{2\sqrt3}{a}=\frac{6K\lambda}{a} \]

Each \(+\lambda\) rod's field points away from it (inward), each at \(120^\circ\) to the other: their resultant is \(2E_0\cos60^\circ=E_0\), along the inward direction of the \(-\lambda\) side. The \(-\lambda\) rod's field is towards the rod (outward, the same direction): \(E_0\). Total:

\[ E_{net}=E_0+E_0=\frac{12K\lambda}{a} \]

Answer: \(E_{net}=\dfrac{12K\lambda}{a}\), directed towards the negatively charged side.

Example 3 · Three infinite line charges along the axes

Infinitely long line charges of density \(\lambda\) lie along the x, y and z axes. Find the magnitude of the electric field at the point \((a,a,a)\).

The point is at distance \(a\sqrt2\) from each axis, so each line gives magnitude \(E_0=\dfrac{2K\lambda}{a\sqrt2}=\dfrac{\sqrt2K\lambda}{a}\), perpendicular to its axis and directed away from it:

\[ \vec E_x=\frac{E_0}{\sqrt2}(\hat j+\hat k),\quad \vec E_y=\frac{E_0}{\sqrt2}(\hat i+\hat k),\quad \vec E_z=\frac{E_0}{\sqrt2}(\hat i+\hat j) \] \[ \vec E_{net}=\frac{E_0}{\sqrt2}\cdot2(\hat i+\hat j+\hat k)=\frac{2K\lambda}{a}(\hat i+\hat j+\hat k) \]

Answer: \(|\vec E_{net}|=\dfrac{2\sqrt3\,K\lambda}{a}\).

Example 4 · Semicircle with λ = λ₀ sinθ

A semicircular wire of radius \(R\) has linear charge density \(\lambda=\lambda_0\sin\theta\), where \(\theta\) is measured from the diameter. Find the electric field at the centre.

\(dq=\lambda R\,d\theta=\lambda_0\sin\theta\,R\,d\theta\). The horizontal components cancel (the density is symmetric about the vertical), leaving \(dE\sin\theta\):

\[ E=\int\frac{K\,dq}{R^2}\sin\theta=\frac{K\lambda_0}{R}\int_0^\pi\sin^2\theta\,d\theta=\frac{K\lambda_0}{R}\cdot\frac\pi2 \]

Answer: \(E=\dfrac{\pi K\lambda_0}{2R}\), along the axis of symmetry (away from the arc for \(\lambda_0>0\)).

Example 5 · Arcs and semi-infinite wires combined

Find the field at the centre \(O\) (radius \(R\), density \(\lambda\)) for the four arrangements in the figure: (a) two semi-infinite straight wires joined by a quarter-circle; (b) a ring with \(+\lambda\) on one half and \(-\lambda\) on the other; (c) a semicircle with two semi-infinite wires; (d) two semicircular rings in perpendicular planes.

(a) Each semi-infinite wire, whose end lies on the perpendicular through \(O\) at distance \(R\), gives \(E_\perp=E_\parallel=\dfrac{K\lambda}R\); the two wires have equal and opposite fields at \(O\) and cancel. The quarter circle gives \(\dfrac{\sqrt2K\lambda}R\) along its bisector. So \(E_{net}=\dfrac{\sqrt2K\lambda}R\).

(b) Each semicircle gives \(\dfrac{2K\lambda}R\). The \(+\lambda\) half pushes the field away from it, the \(-\lambda\) half pulls towards it: both point the same way, \(\vec E=\left(\dfrac{2K\lambda}R+\dfrac{2K\lambda}R\right)(-\hat i)=\dfrac{4K\lambda}R(-\hat i)\) (taking the \(+\lambda\) half on the right).

(c) The two semi-infinite vertical wires: horizontal parts cancel and the parallel parts add to \(2E_0=\dfrac{2K\lambda}R\) downward (\(-\hat j\)) with \(E_0=\dfrac{K\lambda}R\). The semicircle at the bottom gives \(\dfrac{2K\lambda}R\) upward (\(+\hat j\)). Net \(=0\).

(d) Two semicircular rings in perpendicular planes with a common centre: each gives \(\dfrac{2K\lambda}R\), in perpendicular directions: \(\vec E=\dfrac{2K\lambda}R(-\hat i-\hat j)\), so \(|\vec E|=\dfrac{2\sqrt2K\lambda}R\).

Answers: (a) \(\sqrt2K\lambda/R\); (b) \(4K\lambda/R\); (c) \(0\); (d) \(2\sqrt2K\lambda/R\).

Example 6 · Zero field on the axis of two coaxial rings

Two concentric coplanar rings: radius \(R\) with charge \(-Q\) and radius \(3R\) with charge \(+8Q\). At what point \(P\) on the common axis (distance \(x\) from the centre) is the net field zero?

The fields on the axis oppose each other. Setting them equal in magnitude:

\[ \frac{KQx}{(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{K\,8Q\,x}{(9R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\;\Rightarrow\;(9R^2+x^2)^{3/2}=8(R^2+x^2)^{3/2} \]

Taking the \(3/2\) power root: \(9R^2+x^2=4(R^2+x^2)\Rightarrow 3x^2=5R^2\).

Answer: \(x=\sqrt{\dfrac53}\,R\) (and \(x=0\), where both fields vanish).

Force on a charged particle in an external electric field

A point charge \(q\) placed where the electric field is \(\vec E\) experiences

Formula \[ \vec F = q\vec E \]
  • If \(q\) is positive, the force is in the direction of the field.
  • If \(q\) is negative, the force is opposite to the field.
  • A uniform field is represented by parallel, equidistant lines pointing along the field.

Solved examples (charge in a field)

Example 1 · Charge orbiting an infinite line charge

A charged particle \((-q,m)\) moves in a circle of radius \(R\) around an infinite line charge of density \(\lambda\) (perpendicular to the plane of the circle). Find its speed.

\[ F_{el}=F_c\;\Rightarrow\;qE=\frac{mv^2}{R},\quad E=\frac{2K\lambda}{R}\;\Rightarrow\;q\cdot\frac{2K\lambda}R=\frac{mv^2}R \]

Answer: \(v=\sqrt{\dfrac{2Kq\lambda}{m}}\), independent of \(R\) (\(v\neq f(R)\)).

Example 2 · Ball hanging near a large sheet

A ball of mass \(m\) and charge \(q\) hangs by a string next to a large non-conducting sheet of surface density \(\sigma\). Find the angle the string makes with the sheet (vertical).

\[ T\cos\theta=mg,\ T\sin\theta=qE\;\Rightarrow\;\tan\theta=\frac{qE}{mg}=\frac{q\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0mg} \]

Answer: \(\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{\sigma q}{2\varepsilon_0mg}\right)\).

Example 3 · Projectile near a charged sheet: angle for maximum range

A particle of mass \(m\), charge \(q\) is projected with speed \(u\) from the surface of a large sheet of density \(\sigma\) at an angle \(\theta\) with the normal to the sheet. Given \(mg=\dfrac{q\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\) (i.e. \(qE=mg\)), what should \(\theta\) be for maximum range along the normal to the sheet? (The electric force pulls the particle back towards the sheet.)

Vertical: time of flight \(T=\dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g}\). Along the normal, initial speed \(u\cos\theta\) and deceleration \(a=\dfrac{qE}m=g\):

\[ R=u\cos\theta\,T-\frac12gT^2=\frac{2u^2}{g}\sin\theta\cos\theta-\frac{2u^2\sin^2\theta}{g}=\frac{u^2}{g}\left(\sin2\theta+\cos2\theta-1\right) \]

For maximum \(R\): \(\dfrac{dR}{d\theta}=0\Rightarrow2\cos2\theta-2\sin2\theta=0\Rightarrow\tan2\theta=1\Rightarrow2\theta=\dfrac\pi4\).

Answer: \(\theta=\dfrac\pi8\) (\(22.5^\circ\)) with the normal.

Example 4 · Angular acceleration of a charged rod in a field

A rod of mass \(m\) and length \(L\) carries charge \(q\) uniformly distributed along it. It is hinged at one end and hangs vertically. Just after a uniform horizontal electric field \(E_0\) is switched on, find its angular acceleration.

Charge per unit length \(\dfrac qL\). Element at distance \(l\): \(d\tau=E_0\,dq\,l=E_0\dfrac qL\,l\,dl\).

\[ \tau=\int_0^L\frac{E_0q}{L}l\,dl=\frac{E_0qL}{2} \]

(The result is the same as if all charge were concentrated at the centre.) With \(I=\dfrac{mL^2}{3}\) about the hinge:

\[ \alpha=\frac\tau I=\frac{E_0qL/2}{mL^2/3}=\frac{3E_0q}{2mL} \]

Answer: \(\alpha=\dfrac{3E_0q}{2mL}\).

Example 5 · Block and spring in a uniform field

A block of mass \(M\) and charge \(Q\) rests on a smooth horizontal surface, attached to a spring of constant \(k\) (natural length). A horizontal uniform field \(E\) is switched on (along the spring). The block is released from rest with the spring at its natural length. Find the maximum compression/extension and the maximum speed.

Maximum displacement (block momentarily at rest): work by the field = energy stored in the spring:

\[ QEx=\tfrac12kx^2\;\Rightarrow\;x_{max}=\frac{2QE}k \]

Maximum speed occurs at the equilibrium position \(x_0\) where \(kx_0=QE\), i.e. \(x_0=\dfrac{QE}{k}\):

\[ \tfrac12Mv^2=QEx_0-\tfrac12kx_0^2=\frac{(QE)^2}{2k}\;\Rightarrow\;v_{max}=QE\sqrt{\frac1{Mk}} \]

Answer: \(x_{max}=\dfrac{2QE}k\); \(v_{max}=\dfrac{QE}{\sqrt{Mk}}\).

Example 6 · Particle entering a uniform field: deflection and radius of curvature

A particle of charge \(q\) and mass \(m\) enters a region of uniform field \(E\) (plate length \(L\)), perpendicular to the field, with speed \(v_0\). Find (i) the time inside; (ii) its velocity components and the angle \(\delta\) of deflection on leaving; (iii) the radius of curvature of its path at the exit point P.

(i) Horizontal motion is uniform: \(t=\dfrac L{v_0}\).

(ii) \(a=\dfrac{qE}m\), so \(v_y=at=\dfrac{qEL}{mv_0}\) and \(v_x=v_0\):

\[ \tan\delta=\frac{v_y}{v_x}=\frac{qEL}{mv_0^2}=\frac{qEL}{2K_E},\quad K_E=\tfrac12mv_0^2 \]

(iii) At P the speed is \(v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}\). Only the acceleration component perpendicular to the velocity, \(a\cos\delta\), bends the path (\(\cos\delta=v_x/v\)):

\[ R=\frac{v^2}{a\cos\delta}=\frac{v_x^2+v_y^2}{a\cos\delta} \]

Answer: \(t=L/v_0\); \(\tan\delta=qEL/(mv_0^2)\); \(R=\dfrac{v_x^2+v_y^2}{(qE/m)\cos\delta}\). (At entry \(\delta=0\): \(R=\dfrac{mv_0^2}{qE}\).)

Electrostatic Potential Energy

Definition

A very small change in electrostatic potential energy is defined as the negative of the work done by the electrostatic force.

\[ dU = -\,dW_{\text{electrostatic force}} \]

Moving a charge from \(A\) (\(U_A\)) to \(B\) (\(U_B\)) along any path:

\[ \Delta U = \int_A^B dU = -\int_A^B dW_{el}, \qquad U_B - U_A = -\int_A^B \vec F_{el}\cdot d\vec r \]
Formula \[ U_B - U_A = -(W_{el})_{A\to B} \]

Work–energy theorem

\[ W_c + W_{nc} + W_{ext} = \Delta K \]

If \(W_{nc} = 0\) and \(\Delta K = 0\) (the charge is moved very slowly), then \(W_c + W_{ext} = 0\), and since \(W_c = -\Delta U\):

Formula \[ \Delta U = W_{ext} \]

Potential energy of a system of two point charges

Derivation

The work done by the electric force when \(q_2\) moves from distance \(r\) to infinity:

\[ W_{el} = \int_r^\infty \frac{Kq_1q_2}{x^2}\,dx = Kq_1q_2\left[-\frac1x\right]_r^\infty = \frac{Kq_1q_2}{r} \]

With \(\Delta U = -W_{el}\): \(U_\infty - U_r = -\dfrac{Kq_1q_2}{r}\). Taking the reference \(U_\infty = 0\), \(U_r = \dfrac{Kq_1q_2}{r}\).

Formula \[ U = \frac{Kq_1q_2}{r} \]

Take \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) with their proper signs. Standard reference: \(r \to \infty\), \(U = 0\).

Potential energy of a system of n point charges

For \(n\) point charges there are \({}^nC_2\) pairs. The energy of the system is the sum of the energies of all \({}^nC_2\) pairs:

Formula \[ U_{system} = \frac12\sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i\ne j}}^{n}\frac{Kq_iq_j}{r_{ij}} \]

For three charges with separations \(x\) (\(q_1q_2\)), \(y\) (\(q_2q_3\)) and \(z\) (\(q_3q_1\)): \(U = \dfrac{Kq_1q_2}{x} + \dfrac{Kq_2q_3}{y} + \dfrac{Kq_3q_1}{z}\). For four charges at the corners of a square, \(U=\dfrac{Kq_1q_2}{a}+\dfrac{Kq_2q_3}{a}+\dfrac{Kq_3q_4}{a}+\dfrac{Kq_4q_1}{a}+\dfrac{Kq_1q_3}{a\sqrt2}+\dfrac{Kq_2q_4}{a\sqrt2}\).

Solved examples

Example 1 · Three collinear charges with zero total energy

Charges \(q\), \(Q\) and \(3q\) lie on a line with \(q\)–\(Q\) separated by \(a\) and \(Q\)–\(3q\) by \(2a\). Find \(Q\) in terms of \(q\) if the electrostatic potential energy of the system is zero.

\[ U=\frac{KqQ}{a}+\frac{K(3q)Q}{2a}+\frac{Kq(3q)}{3a}=\frac{Kq}{a}\left(Q+\frac{3Q}2+q\right)=0 \]

\(\dfrac{5Q}2=-q\).

Answer: \(Q=-\dfrac{2q}5\).

Example 2 · Energy of a hexagon of alternating charges

Charges \(+q\) and \(-q\) are placed alternately at the six vertices of a regular hexagon of side \(a\). Find the electrostatic potential energy of the system.

There are \({}^6C_2=15\) pairs: 6 adjacent (distance \(a\), unlike → product \(-q^2\)); 6 next-neighbour (distance \(a\sqrt3\), like → \(+q^2\)); 3 opposite (distance \(2a\), unlike → \(-q^2\)).

\[ U=-\frac{6Kq^2}a+\frac{6Kq^2}{a\sqrt3}-\frac{3Kq^2}{2a}=\frac{Kq^2}a\left(-\frac{15}2+\frac6{\sqrt3}\right) \]

Answer: \(U=\dfrac{Kq^2}{a}\left(-\dfrac{15}{2}+\dfrac{6}{\sqrt3}\right)=\dfrac{Kq^2}a\left(-\dfrac{15}2+2\sqrt3\right)\).

Example 3 · Work in moving a charge between two points near a fixed charge

Find the work done (by an external agent) in moving a point charge \(q_0\) from \(A(a,0)\) to \(B(0,b)\) in the field of a fixed charge \(Q\) at the origin.

\[ U_i=\frac{KQq_0}a,\quad U_f=\frac{KQq_0}b,\quad W_{ext}=\Delta U=KQq_0\left(\frac1b-\frac1a\right) \]

Answer: \(W_{ext}=KQq_0\,\dfrac{a-b}{ab}\).

Example 4 · Three identical charged particles at the corners of a triangle

Three particles, each of mass \(m\) and charge \(q\), are at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side \(a\). Find (i) the work done in increasing the side from \(a\) to \(3a\); (ii) the maximum speed if all three are released from rest; (iii) the speed when the side becomes \(3a\).

\(U(a)=3\cdot\dfrac{Kq^2}a=\dfrac{3Kq^2}a\); \(U(3a)=3\cdot\dfrac{Kq^2}{3a}=\dfrac{Kq^2}a\).

(i) \(W_{ext}=\Delta U=\dfrac{Kq^2}{a}-\dfrac{3Kq^2}{a}=-\dfrac{2Kq^2}{a}\) (the electric force does \(+\dfrac{2Kq^2}a\) of work).

(ii) Maximum speed when the particles are infinitely far apart (\(U\to0\)); by symmetry each has speed \(v\): \(\dfrac{3Kq^2}a=3\cdot\tfrac12mv^2\Rightarrow v=\sqrt{\dfrac{2Kq^2}{ma}}\).

(iii) When the side is \(3a\): \(\dfrac{3Kq^2}a=\dfrac{Kq^2}a+3\cdot\tfrac12mv^2\Rightarrow v^2=\dfrac{4Kq^2}{3ma}\).

Answers: (i) \(-\dfrac{2Kq^2}a\); (ii) \(\sqrt{\dfrac{2Kq^2}{ma}}\); (iii) \(\sqrt{\dfrac{4Kq^2}{3ma}}=2\sqrt{\dfrac{Kq^2}{3ma}}\).

Electric Potential (V)

Definition

With the reference at infinity, the electric potential at a point \(P\) is the work done by an external agent in bringing a unit positive charge very slowly from infinity to \(P\).

Formula \[ V_P = \frac{W_{ext}(\infty\to P)}{q} = -\frac{W_{el}(\infty\to P)}{q} = \frac{\Delta U}{q} = \frac{U_P}{q} \] \[ U_P = qV_P \qquad \text{(potential energy = charge} \times \text{potential)} \]

SI unit: \(\text{J C}^{-1}\) or volt. Dimension: \([V] = [\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-3}\,A^{-1}}]\).

Potential due to a point charge

Formula \[ V = \frac{Kq}{r} \]

Take \(q\) with its sign. As \(r \to \infty\), \(V \to 0\).

Note

In the direction of the electric field the potential decreases. A positive charge always moves (on its own) from higher to lower potential; a negative charge moves from lower to higher potential.

Potential on the axis of a charged ring

A charge \(Q\) is distributed on a ring of radius \(R\). All elements are at the same distance \(\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}\) from the axial point \(P\):

\[ dV = \frac{K\,dq}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = \frac{K}{\sqrt{R^2+x^2}}\int dq \]
Formula \[ V = \frac{KQ}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}} \]
  • At the centre (\(x = 0\)): \(V = \dfrac{KQ}{R}\) (maximum). At infinity (\(x \to \infty\)): \(V = 0\).
  • This is valid for a uniform and for a non-uniform charge distribution on the ring.

Some important results

Key results
  • A point charge \(q\) at a point where the potential is \(V\) has potential energy \(U=qV\).
  • Moving \(q\) from \(A\) to \(B\) through any path, with potentials \(V_A,V_B\): \((W_{el})_{A\to B}=-\Delta U=-q\Delta V=-q(V_B-V_A)\) and, if \(W_{nc}=0\), \(\Delta K=0\): \((W_{ext})_{A\to B}=\Delta U=q(V_B-V_A)\).
  • For several charges or bodies, the potential at a point is the scalar sum of the potentials of each.

Potential on the axis of a uniformly charged disc

For the ring of radius \(r\), width \(dr\): \(dQ = 2\pi r\sigma\,dr\) and \(dV = \dfrac{K\,dQ}{\sqrt{r^2 + x^2}}\).

\[ V = K\sigma\,2\pi\int_0^R\frac{r\,dr}{\sqrt{r^2+x^2}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(\sqrt{x^2 + R^2} - x\right) \]
Formula \[ V = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(\sqrt{x^2 + R^2} - x\right), \qquad V_{centre} = \frac{\sigma R}{2\varepsilon_0} \]

Differentiating, \(E = -\dfrac{dV}{dx} = \dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(1 - \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+R^2}}\right)\), the same as the field found earlier.

Potential difference due to an infinite line charge

The field is \(E = \dfrac{2K\lambda}{x}\) at distance \(x\). For points \(A\) and \(B\) at distances \(r_A\) and \(r_B\):

\[ dV = -E\,dx = -\frac{2K\lambda}{x}\,dx \quad\Rightarrow\quad \int_{V_A}^{V_B}dV = -2K\lambda\int_{r_A}^{r_B}\frac{dx}{x} \]
Formula \[ V_B - V_A = -2K\lambda\ln\!\left(\frac{r_B}{r_A}\right) \]

Solved examples

Example 1 · Work to move a charge from the centre of a ring

Find the work done (by an external agent) in moving a charge \(q\) from the centre of a ring of radius \(R\) and charge \(Q\) to (i) infinity, (ii) a point on the axis at distance \(R\sqrt3\) from the centre.

\(V_{centre}=\dfrac{KQ}{R}\); \(V_\infty=0\); \(V(R\sqrt3)=\dfrac{KQ}{\sqrt{R^2+3R^2}}=\dfrac{KQ}{2R}\). Using \(W_{ext}=q(V_f-V_i)\):

\[ \text{(i)}\ W_{ext}=q\left(0-\frac{KQ}R\right)=-\frac{KQq}R,\qquad \text{(ii)}\ W_{ext}=q\left(\frac{KQ}{2R}-\frac{KQ}R\right)=-\frac{KQq}{2R} \]

Answers: (i) \(-\dfrac{KQq}{R}\); (ii) \(-\dfrac{KQq}{2R}\) (the electric force does the opposite sign of work).

Example 2 · Speed from the √3R point to the centre (opposite charges)

A particle \((q,m)\) is released from rest at a point \(P\) on the axis, at \(R\sqrt3\) from the centre of a fixed ring (charge \(Q\), radius \(R\)), \(q\) and \(Q\) being of opposite sign (attraction). Find its speed on reaching the centre.

Energy conservation: gain in kinetic energy = loss in potential energy:

\[ \tfrac12mv^2=\frac{KQq}{R}-\frac{KQq}{2R}=\frac{KQq}{2R}\;\Rightarrow\;v=\sqrt{\frac{KQq}{mR}} \]

Answer: \(v=\sqrt{\dfrac{KQq}{mR}}\) (with magnitudes of \(Q\) and \(q\)).

Example 3 · Minimum speed so the particle does not return

A ring (charge \(Q\), radius \(R\)) is fixed. A particle \((+q,m)\) of the same sign as \(Q\) is at the axial point \(P\) at distance \(R\sqrt3\) from the centre \(O\). What minimum speed \(v_0\) must be given to the particle towards \(O\) so that it does not return to \(P\)?

Along the axis the potential energy is largest at the centre \(O\) (\(U_O=\dfrac{KQq}R\)), so the particle just needs to reach \(O\) with zero speed. Mechanical energy conservation between \(P\) and \(O\):

\[ \tfrac12mv_0^2+\frac{KQq}{2R}=0+\frac{KQq}R\;\Rightarrow\;v_0=\sqrt{\frac{KQq}{mR}} \]

Answer: \(v_0=\sqrt{\dfrac{KQq}{mR}}\).

Example 4 · Potential of an infinite alternating chain of charges

Charges \(+q\) are at \(x_0,3x_0,5x_0,\dots\) and charges \(-q\) at \(2x_0,4x_0,6x_0,\dots\) on a line. Find the potential at the origin.

\[ V=\frac{Kq}{x_0}+\frac{Kq}{3x_0}+\frac{Kq}{5x_0}+\dots-\left(\frac{Kq}{2x_0}+\frac{Kq}{4x_0}+\dots\right)=\frac{Kq}{x_0}\left(1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\dots\right) \]

Using \(\ln(1+x)=x-\dfrac{x^2}2+\dfrac{x^3}3-\dots\) at \(x=1\), the series equals \(\ln2\).

Answer: \(V=\dfrac{Kq\ln2}{x_0}\).

Example 5 · Work between the centres of two coaxial rings

Two identical coaxial rings of radius \(R\), with charges \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\), have their centres a distance \(R\) apart (so each point of one ring is at \(\sqrt2R\) from the other's centre). Find the work done in moving a charge \(q\) slowly from the centre of ring 1 to the centre of ring 2.

\(V_{O_1}=\dfrac{KQ_1}R+\dfrac{KQ_2}{\sqrt2R}\), \(V_{O_2}=\dfrac{KQ_2}R+\dfrac{KQ_1}{\sqrt2R}\).

\[ W_{ext}=q(V_{O_2}-V_{O_1})=\frac{qK}{R}\left(Q_2-Q_1\right)\left(1-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)=\frac{q(Q_2-Q_1)(\sqrt2-1)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R\sqrt2} \]

Answer: \(W=\dfrac{q(Q_2-Q_1)(\sqrt2-1)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\sqrt2\,R}\).

Example 6 · Locus of zero potential for charges Q and −2Q

A charge \(Q\) is at \((3a,0)\) and a charge \(-2Q\) at \((-3a,0)\). Find the locus of points of zero potential in the plane.

For \(V=0\) at \((x,y)\): \(\dfrac{KQ}{\sqrt{(3a-x)^2+y^2}}=\dfrac{2KQ}{\sqrt{(3a+x)^2+y^2}}\Rightarrow(3a+x)^2+y^2=4\left[(3a-x)^2+y^2\right]\).

\[ 9a^2+6ax+x^2+y^2=36a^2-24ax+4x^2+4y^2\;\Rightarrow\;3x^2+3y^2-30ax+27a^2=0 \]

Answer: \(x^2+y^2-10ax+9a^2=0\), i.e. \((x-5a)^2+y^2=(4a)^2\): a circle of centre \((5a,0)\) and radius \(4a\).

Relation Between Field and Potential

For a small displacement \(d\vec l\) of a charge \(q\) by the electric force, \(dW_{el} = q\vec E\cdot d\vec l\), and also \(dW_{el} = -q\,dV\). Hence

Formula \[ dV = -\vec E\cdot d\vec l, \qquad dV = -|E||dl|\cos\theta \]

\(\theta\) is the angle between \(\vec E\) and \(d\vec l\).

  • \(\theta = 90^\circ\) (\(\vec E \perp d\vec l\)): \(dV = 0\), the potential is constant.
  • \(E = 0\): \(dV = 0\), the potential is constant.
  • \(\theta = 0^\circ\): \(dV = -E\,dl\), the potential decreases moving along the field.
  • \(\theta = 180^\circ\): \(dV = +E\,dl\), the potential increases moving against the field.

Integrating, \(V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B\vec E\cdot d\vec l\). In a closed loop the integral of the electric field is zero (\(\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0\)): the integral of the electric field is path independent.

Change in potential when the field is given

If \(\vec E = E_x\hat i + E_y\hat j + E_z\hat k\) and \(d\vec l = dx\,\hat i + dy\,\hat j + dz\,\hat k\):

Formula \[ dV = -\left[E_x\,dx + E_y\,dy + E_z\,dz\right], \qquad \Delta V = V_B - V_A = -\int_{(x_1,y_1,z_1)}^{(x_2,y_2,z_2)}\left(E_x\,dx + E_y\,dy + E_z\,dz\right) \]

Electric field vector when the potential is given

If the potential \(V(x,y,z)\) is known, \(dV = -\vec E\cdot d\vec l\) gives

Formula \[ \vec E = -\nabla V, \qquad \nabla = \hat i\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat j\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat k\frac{\partial}{\partial z} \] \[ \vec E = -\left[\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\hat i + \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\hat j + \frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\hat k\right] \]

For a field depending on one variable only, \(E = -\dfrac{dV}{dx}\) (or \(-\dfrac{dV}{dr}\) for a radial field).

Relation in a uniform electric field

For a uniform field, \(\vec E\) comes out of the integral: \(dV = -\vec E\cdot\!\int d\vec l\), and by the polygon law of vector addition \(\int_A^B d\vec l = \vec{AB}\):

Formula \[ V_B - V_A = -\vec E\cdot\vec{AB} = -E\,d \]

\(d\) is the distance between \(A\) and \(B\) measured in the direction of the electric field.

Equipotential Surfaces

Definition

An equipotential surface is a surface on which the potential is the same at every point.

  • It is always perpendicular to the direction of the electric field.
  • Shape: spherical for a point charge, cylindrical for an infinitely long line charge, and parallel planes for an infinite sheet of charge.
  • Two equipotential surfaces never intersect each other.
Potential difference between two equipotential surfaces
  • Point charge: \(V_2-V_1=\dfrac{Kq}{r_2}-\dfrac{Kq}{r_1}\), with \(E=\dfrac{Kq}{r^2}\).
  • Infinite line charge: \(V_2-V_1=-2K\lambda\ln\dfrac{r_2}{r_1}\), with \(E=\dfrac{2K\lambda}{d}\).
  • Infinite sheet: \(\vec E=\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\hat n\) and \(V_2-V_1=-Ed\).

Solved examples

Equipotential lines of 100 V, 80 V and 60 V are drawn as shown (slanting lines). Points A and B lie on the 80 V and 60 V... find the magnitude and direction of the electric field. (Reading from the figure: A and B are joined by a path perpendicular to the lines, the perpendicular spacing of the lines is 4 m between the 80 V and 60 V lines, so \(V_B-V_A=20\) V along the path A→B.)

Field is perpendicular to the equipotentials and directed towards lower potential; \(|E|=\dfrac{|\Delta V|}{\Delta l}\):

\[ V_B-V_A=-E\,d\;\Rightarrow\;20=E\times4\;\Rightarrow\;E=5\ \text{V/m} \]

Answer: \(E=5\) V/m, perpendicular to the lines, i.e. \(\vec E=4\hat i-3\hat j\) in the PDF's units (a vector of magnitude 5). (Sign/direction as noted in the PDF; figure partly unreadable.)

Example 2 · Point charge from spaced equipotential arcs

Concentric equipotential arcs of a point charge are drawn with potentials \(60,30,20\) V (radially increasing). The gap between two arcs is \(0.10\) m with potential difference \(30\) V. Find the field there and relate it to the charge.

\[ V_A-V_B=-E\cdot d\;\Rightarrow\;30=0.10\,E\;\Rightarrow\;E=300\ \text{V/m} \]

For a point charge \(E=\dfrac{Kq}{r^2}\), so \(Kq=E\,r^2\) at the radius \(r\) where this field is measured.

Electric Dipole

Definition

A dipole is a system of two equal and opposite point charges separated by some distance (generally this distance is very small).

Electric dipole moment

  • It is a vector quantity whose magnitude equals the product of the magnitude of one point charge and the separation between the two.
  • Its direction is from the negative charge to the positive charge.
  • Unit: C·m.
Formula \[ \vec p = q \times \vec d, \qquad p = q(2l) \]

For a system of several charges, the net dipole moment is the vector sum of the dipole moments of its pairs.

Electric field and potential due to a dipole

Take a dipole with moment \(p\) whose charges \(\pm q\) are separated by \(2l\). We find the field and potential at distance \(r\) from the centre.

Derivation (axial point)

For a point on the axis, \(r_{+} = r - l\) from \(+q\) and \(r_{-} = r + l\) from \(-q\):

\[ E = \frac{Kq}{(r-l)^2} - \frac{Kq}{(r+l)^2} = \frac{4Kqrl}{(r^2 - l^2)^2} = \frac{2Kpr}{(r^2-l^2)^2} \]
Axial point (\(\theta = 0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\)) \[ E_{axial} = \frac{2Kpr}{(r^2 - l^2)^2} \ \xrightarrow{\,r\gg l\,}\ \frac{2Kp}{r^3} \quad(\text{along } \vec p) \] \[ V_{axial} = \frac{Kp}{r^2 - l^2} \ \xrightarrow{\,r\gg l\,}\ \frac{Kp}{r^2} \]
Equatorial point (\(\theta = 90^\circ\)) \[ E_{eq} = \frac{Kp}{(r^2 + l^2)^{3/2}} \ \xrightarrow{\,r\gg l\,}\ \frac{Kp}{r^3} \quad(\text{opposite to } \vec p) \] \[ V_{eq} = 0 \quad(\text{equal and opposite potentials}) \]

So for \(r \gg l\), \(E_{axial} = 2E_{equatorial}\).

General point \((r,\theta)\) for a short dipole \[ E_r = \frac{2Kp\cos\theta}{r^3}, \qquad E_\perp = \frac{Kp\sin\theta}{r^3} \] \[ E_{net} = \frac{Kp}{r^3}\sqrt{1 + 3\cos^2\theta}, \qquad \tan\alpha = \frac{\tan\theta}{2} \] \[ V = \frac{Kp\cos\theta}{r^2} \]

\(E_r\) is the radial component, \(E_\perp\) the component perpendicular to \(\vec r\), and \(\alpha\) the angle that \(\vec E\) makes with the radial direction.

Equatorial plane: the potential is zero everywhere on it.

Behaviour of a dipole in a uniform electric field

A dipole of moment \(\vec p\) is placed in a uniform field \(\vec E\) with angle \(\theta\) between them.

  • The net force on the dipole in a uniform field is always zero.
  • The torque on the dipole is
Formula \[ \tau = pE\sin\theta, \qquad \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E \]

(Direction by the right-hand thumb rule.)

  • The dipole is in stable equilibrium when \(\theta = 0^\circ\) (\(\vec p\) along \(\vec E\)).
  • The equilibrium is unstable when \(\theta = 180^\circ\) (\(\vec p\) opposite to \(\vec E\)).

Work done, potential energy and oscillation

Work done in rotating a dipole

The external torque equals the electric torque in magnitude, so \(W_{ext} = \int\tau\,d\theta = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}pE\sin\theta\,d\theta\).

Formulas \[ (W_{ext})_{\theta_1\to\theta_2} = pE(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2), \qquad (W_{el})_{\theta_1\to\theta_2} = pE(\cos\theta_2 - \cos\theta_1) \] \[ (W_{ext})_{0\to\theta} = pE(1 - \cos\theta) \]

Change in potential energy: \(\Delta U = W_{ext}\), so \(U_{\theta_2} - U_{\theta_1} = pE(\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2)\).

If the reference of potential energy is taken at \(\theta = 90^\circ\) (\(U = 0\) there), then at angle \(\theta\):

Formula \[ U_\theta = -pE\cos\theta = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

Minimum at \(\theta = 0^\circ\) (\(-pE\)), maximum at \(\theta = 180^\circ\) (\(+pE\)).

Small oscillations. For \(\theta \ll 1\), \(\tau = -pE\sin\theta \approx -pE\theta\). Since \(\tau \propto -\theta\), the motion is angular SHM:

Formula \[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{pE}{I}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{pE}} \]

\(I\) is the moment of inertia of the dipole about the axis through its centre.

Force on a dipole in a non-uniform electric field

Let a short dipole lie along the \(x\)-axis in a non-uniform field directed along \(x\); the field is \(E\) at \(-q\) and \(E + dE\) at \(+q\) (separation \(dx\)).

\[ F = \left|-Eq + (E + dE)q\right| = |q\,dE|, \qquad p = q\,dx \]
Formula \[ F = \left|p\,\frac{dE}{dx}\right|, \qquad \text{or}\quad F = -\frac{dU}{dx},\ U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

Alternate method: \(F=-\dfrac{dU}{dx}=+\dfrac{d(pE\cos\theta)}{dx}=\dfrac{d(pE)}{dx}\) (for \(\vec p\) along the field).

Charges \(+q\), \(+q\) and \(-2q\) are placed at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side \(a\). Find the net dipole moment.

Pair the \(-2q\) charge with each \(+q\): \(p_1=p_2=qa\), angle \(60^\circ\) between them.

Charges \(+3q\) at \((0,a)\), \(-2q\) at \((-a,0)\), \(+q\) at \((a,0)\) and \(-2q\) at \((0,-a)\). Find the net dipole moment.

A ring of radius \(R\) carries \(+\lambda\) on its upper half and \(-\lambda\) on its lower half. Find its dipole moment.

\[ p=\int_0^\pi2\lambda R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta=2R^2\lambda\,[-\cos\theta]_0^\pi=4\lambda R^2 \]

Example 4 · Work in moving a charge near a dipole

A dipole \(p\) is at the origin. A charge \(q\) is moved very slowly from \(A\) (distance \(r\), angle \(60^\circ\) with \(\vec p\)) to \(B\) (distance \(2r\), angle \(120^\circ\)). Find the work done by the external agent.

\[ W_{ext}=q\,\Delta V=qKp\left(\frac{\cos120^\circ}{(2r)^2}-\frac{\cos60^\circ}{r^2}\right)=qKp\left(-\frac1{8r^2}-\frac1{2r^2}\right)=-\frac{5Kpq}{8r^2} \]

Example 5 · Field and potential at A due to two dipoles

Two dipoles of equal moment \(p\) are placed at distance \(a\) on either side of a point \(A\): dipole ② lies on the axis through A (A is axial for it), dipole ③ has A on its equatorial line. Find the field and potential at \(A\).

Axial field of ②: \(\dfrac{2Kp}{a^3}\hat i\). Equatorial field of ③: \(\dfrac{Kp}{a^3}\), opposite to its moment: \(-\dfrac{Kp}{a^3}\hat j\). Potentials: axial \(\dfrac{Kp}{a^2}\), equatorial \(0\).

\[ \vec E_A=\frac{Kp}{a^3}\left(2\hat i-\hat j\right),\quad |\vec E_A|=\frac{\sqrt5\,Kp}{a^3},\quad V_A=\frac{Kp}{a^2} \]

Answer: \(|\vec E_A|=\sqrt5Kp/a^3\); \(V_A=Kp/a^2\).

Example 6 · Angle at which the field is perpendicular to p

If the electric field at a point \(A\) of a dipole is perpendicular to the direction of the dipole moment, find the angle \(\theta\) of \(A\) with \(\vec p\).

E is perpendicular to \(\vec p\), so \(\alpha+\theta=90^\circ\) (\(\alpha\) = angle of \(\vec E\) with the radial direction). With \(\tan\alpha=\dfrac{\tan\theta}2\):

\[ \tan(90^\circ-\theta)=\frac{\tan\theta}2\;\Rightarrow\;\cot\theta=\frac{\tan\theta}2\;\Rightarrow\;\tan^2\theta=2 \]

Answer: \(\theta=\tan^{-1}\sqrt2\) (first quadrant). In the second quadrant \(\theta=\pi-\tan^{-1}\sqrt2\); similarly in the other two quadrants (4 locations in all).

Example 7 · Force between a point charge and a dipole

A dipole \(\vec p\) lies on the axis through a point charge \(q\) at distance \(r\) (the dipole is along the line to the charge, \(\vec p\) along the field of \(q\)). Find the force on the dipole.

\(E=\dfrac{Kq}{r^2}\), \(\dfrac{dE}{dr}=-\dfrac{2Kq}{r^3}\):

\[ F=\left|p\frac{dE}{dr}\right|=\frac{2Kpq}{r^3} \]

Answer: \(F=\dfrac{2Kpq}{r^3}\), attractive, \(F\propto\dfrac1{r^3}\).

Example 8 · Force between two coaxial dipoles

Two dipoles \(\vec p_1\) and \(\vec p_2\), parallel and on the same axis at distance \(r\). Find the magnitude and direction of the force exerted by \(p_1\) on \(p_2\).

Field of \(p_1\) on its axis: \(\dfrac{2Kp_1}{r^3}\). Energy of \(p_2\): \(U=-\vec p_2\cdot\vec E=-\dfrac{2Kp_1p_2}{r^3}\).

\[ F=-\frac{dU}{dr}=-\frac{d}{dr}\left(-\frac{2Kp_1p_2}{r^3}\right)=\frac{2Kp_1p_2(-3)}{r^4}=-\frac{6Kp_1p_2}{r^4} \]

Answer: \(|F|=\dfrac{6Kp_1p_2}{r^4}\), attractive (towards decreasing \(r\)), \(F\propto\dfrac1{r^4}\).

Example 9 · Force when p₂ makes an angle θ

Dipole \(p_1\) produces the field; dipole \(p_2\) lies along the radial direction at distance \(r\) and angle \(\theta\) from the axis of \(p_1\). Find the radial force on \(p_2\).

The radial field of \(p_1\) is \(\dfrac{2Kp_1\cos\theta}{r^3}\) and \(U=-\vec p_2\cdot\vec E=-\dfrac{2Kp_1p_2\cos\theta}{r^3}\):

\[ F=-\frac{dU}{dr}=-\frac{6Kp_1p_2\cos\theta}{r^4} \]

Answer: \(F=-\dfrac{6Kp_1p_2\cos\theta}{r^4}\) (magnitude \(\dfrac{6Kp_1p_2\cos\theta}{r^4}\), attractive).

Electric Lines of Force (Electric Field Lines)

Definition

Electric lines of force (ELOF) are imaginary curves drawn in a region of electric field such that the tangent drawn at any point of these lines gives the direction of the electric field at that point.

Properties of ELOF

  1. Two ELOF can never intersect. Otherwise there would be two directions of the field at one point.
  2. ELOF are always perpendicular to equipotential curves, and the potential decreases in the direction of the ELOF.
  3. ELOF start from a positive charge and end on a negative charge.
  4. Point charge: radial lines (outward for positive, inward for negative). Dipole: curves from + to − (see the figure under point charge).
  5. The magnitude of the field is greater where the lines are closer: if lines near \(A\) are closer than near \(B\), \(|\vec E_A|>|\vec E_B|\) (and potential \(V_A>V_B\) or \(<\) according to direction).
  6. ELOF due to an electrostatic field never form a closed loop, because of the conservative nature of the field (\(\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l=0\)).
  7. The number of ELOF originating or terminating on a point charge is directly proportional to its magnitude: \(N_{ELOF}\propto|q|\); for two charges \(\dfrac{|q_1|}{|q_2|}=\dfrac{N_1}{N_2}\).
  8. For a charged conductor in electrostatic condition, all ELOF are perpendicular to the surface (the surface is equipotential).
  9. Inside the material of a conductor in electrostatic condition, \(E=0\) so no ELOF exist inside; the whole conductor is at constant potential.
  10. If the field in a region is uniform, the ELOF are parallel and equidistant lines.

Area Vector

Definition

For a given surface, the area vector is a vector quantity whose magnitude equals the area of the surface and whose direction is perpendicular to the surface.

\[ \vec S = S\,\hat n, \qquad d\vec S = dS\,\hat n \]
  • For a closed surface such as a sphere or a cube, the direction of the area vector is always taken along the outward normal.
  • For a flat surface, either normal can be used; fix one and keep it throughout.

Electric Flux

  • It is a scalar quantity.
  • It is a measure of how many electric lines of force pass perpendicularly through a given surface.
  • For a surface in an electric field, the flux passing through (associated with, linked with) the surface is the dot product of the electric field vector and the area vector.
  • Unit: \(\dfrac{\text{N m}^2}{\text{C}}\) or \(\text{V m}\).
Uniform field \[ \phi = \vec E\cdot\vec S = ES\cos\theta \]

\(\theta\) is the angle between \(\vec E\) and \(\vec S\).

Non-uniform field \[ d\phi = d\vec S\cdot\vec E, \qquad \phi_{total} = \int_S d\vec S\cdot\vec E \]

A very small flux passes through a small area \(d\vec S\); add them over the whole surface.

Flux of a point charge through a sphere

For charge \(q\) at the centre of a sphere of radius \(R\): \(d\phi=\vec E\cdot d\vec S=\dfrac{q\,dS}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}\), so \(\phi_{total}=\int d\phi=\dfrac q{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}\cdot4\pi R^2=\dfrac q{\varepsilon_0}\).

Closed surfaces and sign of flux

Flux should be taken with its sign.

  1. If the angle between \(\vec E\) and \(d\vec S\) is acute, lines come out of the surface and the flux is positive. If ELOF rise out from a closed surface, the net charge inside is positive: \(\phi=+\dfrac q{\varepsilon_0}\).
  2. If the angle between \(\vec E\) and \(d\vec S\) is obtuse, lines go inside and the flux is negative; the net charge inside is negative: \(\phi=-\dfrac q{\varepsilon_0}\).
  3. If a charge is outside a closed surface, the flux entering equals the flux leaving: net flux zero (\(\phi_{in}=-\phi_{out}\)).

If \(\phi_1\) and \(\phi_2\) are the fluxes entering and leaving a closed surface, then \(-\phi_1+\phi_2=\dfrac q{\varepsilon_0}\), i.e. \(q_{in}=\varepsilon_0(\phi_2-\phi_1)\).

Note

The flux associated with any closed surface is independent of the positions of the charges enclosed within it. However, on changing the positions of the enclosed charges, the flux linked with a part of the Gaussian surface may change.

Electric flux through the base of a cone

A point charge \(q\) at the vertex of a right circular cone of height \(H\), base radius \(R\), semi-conical angle \(\theta\).

Derivation

Take a ring of radius \(r\) and width \(dr\) on the base. The field there is \(\dfrac{Kq}{H^2+r^2}\) and its angle with the normal \(\alpha\) has \(\cos\alpha=\dfrac H{\sqrt{H^2+r^2}}\):

\[ d\phi=\vec E\cdot d\vec S=\frac{Kq}{H^2+r^2}\,2\pi r\,dr\cos\alpha=\frac{q}{2\varepsilon_0}\frac{H\,r\,dr}{(H^2+r^2)^{3/2}} \]

Put \(H^2+r^2=t\), \(r\,dr=dt/2\); integrate from \(r=0\) to \(R\):

\[ \phi=\frac{qH}{2\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac1H-\frac1{\sqrt{H^2+R^2}}\right]=\frac q{2\varepsilon_0}\left[1-\frac H{\sqrt{H^2+R^2}}\right] \]
Formula \[ \phi_{base}=\frac q{2\varepsilon_0}\left[1-\cos\theta\right] \]

Result: the flux through the cone's base equals the flux going out through the cone (any surface across the same cone).

If two cones with charges \(q_1\) (semi-angle \(\alpha\)) and \(q_2\) (semi-angle \(\beta\)) get equal fluxes: \(\phi_1=\phi_2\):

\[ \frac{q_1}{2\varepsilon_0}(1-\cos\alpha)=\frac{q_2}{2\varepsilon_0}(1-\cos\beta)\Rightarrow\frac{q_1}{q_2}\sin^2\frac\alpha2=\sin^2\frac\beta2\Rightarrow\beta=2\sin^{-1}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{q_1}{q_2}}\sin\frac\alpha2\right) \]

Standard cases

Key results
  • Charge at the centre of a cube: \(\phi_{cube}=\dfrac q{\varepsilon_0}\); each face \(\dfrac q{6\varepsilon_0}\).
  • Charge at a corner of a cube (small ball with centre at a vertex): \(\dfrac18\) of the ball lies inside; flux \(\dfrac q{8\varepsilon_0}\); the three faces touching the charge get zero flux; the other three get \(\dfrac q{24\varepsilon_0}\) each.
  • Charge at the centre of an edge: \(\dfrac14\) of the ball is inside the cube.
  • Charge at the centre of a face: \(\dfrac12\) of the ball is inside the cube.
  • Square of side \(a\), charge at height \(a/2\) above its centre: \(\phi=\dfrac q{6\varepsilon_0}\); a square of side \(2a\) at distance \(a\): \(\dfrac q{6\varepsilon_0}\), each quarter (side \(a\)) \(\dfrac q{24\varepsilon_0}\).
  • Open hemisphere with \(q\) at the centre of the flat face: \(\phi_{hemisphere}=\dfrac q{2\varepsilon_0}\) (complete the sphere, \(\phi=\dfrac q{\varepsilon_0}\), shared equally).

Gauss's Law

Statement

According to this theorem, the total electric flux passing through any closed surface (called a Gaussian surface) is equal to \(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\) times the total charge inside the closed surface.

Formula \[ \phi_{total} = \oint\vec E\cdot d\vec S = \frac{q_{in}}{\varepsilon_0} \]

For charges \(+q_1, -q_2, +q_3\) inside: \(\phi = \dfrac{q_1 - q_2 + q_3}{\varepsilon_0}\).

  • The theorem is applicable only for a closed surface.
  • It helps to find the magnitude of the electric field due to certain types of charge distribution: a point charge, an infinitely long line charge, an infinitely large sheet of charge and a spherical charge distribution.
  • Only the enclosed charge \(q_{in}\) appears on the right side; the field \(\vec E\) on the left is due to all charges, inside and outside.

The law in differential form is given in charge density from field.

Application of Gauss's Law

In certain types of charge distribution we can find the magnitude of the electric field if its direction is known. Suppose at every point of the Gaussian surface the magnitude of the field is constant and the angle between the field and the area vector is zero everywhere (or the field is perpendicular to the area vector on the other parts of the surface). Then:

\[ E\,S = \frac{q_{in}}{\varepsilon_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = \frac{q_{in}}{\varepsilon_0 S} \]

Cavity problem and overlapping spheres

A cavity inside a uniformly charged body is treated by superposition: the body without a cavity (density \(+\rho\)) plus a body of density \(-\rho\) filling the cavity.

Spherical cavity \[ \vec E_P = \vec E_1 - \vec E_2 = \frac{\rho\vec r_1}{3\varepsilon_0} - \frac{\rho\vec r_2}{3\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}(\vec r_1 - \vec r_2) = \frac{\rho\,\vec d}{3\varepsilon_0} \]
Cylindrical cavity \[ \vec E_P = \frac{\rho\,\vec d}{2\varepsilon_0} \]

\(\vec d\) is the vector from the centre of the solid sphere (or cylinder axis) to the centre of the cavity. The field at every point inside the cavity is the same (uniform).

Two overlapping spheres (+ρ and −ρ) \[ \vec E_P = \frac{\rho\vec r_1}{3\varepsilon_0} + \frac{(-\rho)\vec r_2}{3\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\rho\,\vec a}{3\varepsilon_0} \]

\(\vec a\) is the vector from the centre of the positive sphere to the centre of the negative sphere; the field in the overlapping region is uniform.

Charge density from a known field (differential form)

Take a thin slab of cross-section \(S\) and thickness \(dx\) where the field changes from \(E\) to \(E+dE\). The net outward flux is \((E+dE)S-ES=S\,dE\) and equals \(\dfrac{\rho S\,dx}{\varepsilon_0}\). Hence for variation along one direction:

Formula \[ \rho = \varepsilon_0\frac{dE}{dx} \]
Divergence form of Gauss's law \[ \nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial z} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \]
Note

Conductors in Electrostatics

Properties of a conductor

  1. Inside the material of a conducting body the electric field is zero.

    Reason: a conductor has a large number of free electrons. If any field existed inside, the free electrons would experience an electric force and start moving, which is not possible in electrostatic condition.

  2. Any excess charge given to a conductor resides on its outer surface.

    Reason: take a Gaussian surface just inside the surface; \(\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec S=\dfrac{q_{in}}{\varepsilon_0}\) with \(E=0\) gives \(q_{in}=0\). For a charge \(+q\) in a cavity of a neutral conductor, a Gaussian surface in the material gives \(0=q-q'\), so the cavity wall carries \(-q'\) with \(q'=q\).

  3. Electric potential is uniform everywhere on the surface and in the volume of a conductor.

    Reason: if the potential were not uniform, charges would start moving from one place to another, which is not possible in an electrostatic situation.

  4. The electric field is always normal to the surface of a conductor.

    Reason: the surface is equipotential, and the field is always normal to an equipotential surface.

  5. The electric field intensity near the surface of a conductor is \(\dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\), where \(\sigma\) is the local surface charge density.

    Take a small pillbox across the surface: flux goes out only through the outer face (inside \(E=0\), the sides contribute nothing): \(E\,dS=\dfrac{\sigma\,dS}{\varepsilon_0}\), so \(E=\dfrac\sigma{\varepsilon_0}\). Of this, the small local patch gives \(\dfrac\sigma{2\varepsilon_0}\) on each side, and the rest of the conductor supplies the other \(\dfrac\sigma{2\varepsilon_0}\) (outside; inside the two cancel). The field acting on the charge element itself is therefore \(\dfrac\sigma{2\varepsilon_0}\).

Electrostatic pressure

The force on a small surface charge element \(\sigma\,dS\) is \(F = qE = \sigma\,dS\cdot\dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\). The force per unit area is the electrostatic pressure:

Formula \[ P = \frac{F}{dS} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac12\varepsilon_0\left(\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\right)^2 = \frac12\varepsilon_0E^2 \]

Electrostatic pressure equals the electrostatic potential energy density near the surface; it acts outward.

Surface charge density and radius of curvature

The surface charge density at a point on a conductor is inversely proportional to the radius of curvature at that point.

Two spheres joined by a long wire

Spheres of radii \(R_1, R_2\) with charges \(q_1,q_2\). The wire is very long, so the potential at each sphere is due almost entirely to its own charge. When the switch is closed, \(V_A=V_B\):

\[ \frac{Kq_1}{r_1}=\frac{Kq_2}{r_2}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{\sigma_14\pi r_1^2}{r_1}=\frac{\sigma_24\pi r_2^2}{r_2}\;\Rightarrow\;\sigma_1r_1=\sigma_2r_2 \]
Result \[ \sigma r = \text{constant}, \qquad \sigma \propto \frac1r, \qquad \frac{q_1}{q_2} = \frac{r_1}{r_2} \]

For an irregular conductor, \(\sigma\) is largest at the sharpest points (smallest \(r\)): \(r_3>r_2>r_1\) gives \(\sigma_3<\sigma_2<\sigma_1\). This is an approximate result.

Earthed conductor and induced charges

  • The potential of an earthed conductor is zero (\(V_{conductor} = V_{earth} = 0\)).
  • The charge of an isolated conductor is constant.

Field and potential due to induced charges. Inside a conductor \(E_{inside}=0\) and \(V\) is constant. For an external point charge \(q\), the field due to the induced charges at an inner point is the negative of the field of \(q\):

\[ \vec E_{net}=0\Rightarrow\vec E_q+\vec E_{in}=0\Rightarrow\vec E_{induced}=-\vec E_q=-\frac{Kq}{x_2^2}\hat n \]

Potentials at the centre \(O\) and any inner point \(P\) are equal: \(V_{ind}(O)+\dfrac{Kq}{d}=V_{ind}(P)+\dfrac{Kq}{x_2}\). For a neutral spherical conductor \(V_{ind}(O)=0\), so

Formula \[ V_{induced}(P)=Kq\left[\frac1d-\frac1{x_2}\right] \]

(\(d\) = distance of \(q\) from the centre, \(x_2\) = distance of \(P\) from \(q\).) For an initially charged isolated sphere (charge \(Q\)) with an external charge \(q\) at distance \(d\), the potential at the centre is \(V_O=\dfrac{KQ}R+\dfrac{Kq}d\) (the induced charges add nothing at the centre, being on the surface at distance \(R\) with zero net).

Earthed sphere near a charge

An earthed conducting sphere of radius \(R\) with a point charge \(q\) at distance \(x\) from the centre: the potential at the centre is zero, and all induced charge lies at distance \(R\) from the centre:

\[ \frac{Kq}{x}+\frac{Kq_{ind}}{R}=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad q_{ind}=-\frac{qR}{x} \]

If the external charge moves closer with speed \(v\) (\(-\dfrac{dx}{dt}=v\)), charge flows to or from the earth. The magnitude of the current: \(I=\dfrac{dq_{ind}}{dt}=\dfrac{QR}{x^2}\,v\), and its direction is the direction of flow of positive charge.

Solved examples (induced charges)

Example 1 · Ratio of induced potentials at two surface points

A neutral conducting sphere of radius \(R\) has a point charge \(q\) at distance \(2R\) from its centre \(O\) on the left and a charge \(2q\) at distance \(3R\) on the right (both on the horizontal line through \(O\)). A is the point of the sphere on the right end of the horizontal diameter and B is the lowest point of the sphere. Find \(|V_A/V_B|\) due to the induced charges.

All points of the conductor are at the same total potential, and \(V_{ind}(O)=0\) for a neutral sphere. At \(O\): \(V=\dfrac{Kq}{2R}+\dfrac{2Kq}{3R}\).

Point A (distances \(3R\) from \(q\), \(2R\) from \(2q\)): \(V_{ind}(A)+\dfrac{Kq}{3R}+\dfrac{2Kq}{2R}=\dfrac{Kq}{2R}+\dfrac{2Kq}{3R}\):

\[ V_{ind}(A)=\frac{Kq}R\left(\frac12+\frac23-\frac13-1\right)=-\frac{Kq}{6R} \]

Point B (distances \(\sqrt5R\) from \(q\), \(\sqrt{10}R\) from \(2q\)): \(V_{ind}(B)+\dfrac{Kq}{\sqrt5R}+\dfrac{2Kq}{\sqrt{10}R}=\dfrac{Kq}{2R}+\dfrac{2Kq}{3R}\):

\[ V_{ind}(B)=\frac{Kq}R\left[\frac76-\frac{1+\sqrt2}{\sqrt5}\right] \]

Answer: \(\dfrac{V_A}{V_B}=\dfrac{-\frac16}{\frac76-\frac{1+\sqrt2}{\sqrt5}}\approx-1.9\).

Example 2 · Current into an earthed sphere as a charge approaches

A charge of magnitude \(Q\) moves towards the centre of an earthed conducting sphere of radius \(R\) along a straight line with speed \(v\). Find the induced charge when it is at distance \(x\) from the centre and the current flowing to or from the earth.

The sphere is grounded: \(V_{conductor}=V_{centre}=0\): \(\dfrac{KQ}{x}-\dfrac{Kq}{R}=0\), so the induced charge has magnitude \(q=\dfrac{QR}x=QR\left(\dfrac1x\right)\).

\[ \frac{dq}{dt}=QR\,\frac{d(1/x)}{dt}=-\frac{QR}{x^2}\frac{dx}{dt},\qquad I=\frac{QR}{x^2}\,v\quad\left(-\frac{dx}{dt}=v\right) \]

Answer: \(q=\dfrac{QR}x\); \(I=\dfrac{QRv}{x^2}\); the current is the direction of flow of positive charge, here from the conductor to the earth (for a negative external charge all +ve induced charge flows into the earth).

Charge distribution on parallel plane sheets

For large parallel conducting plates (thickness \(t\) very small), the charge on each plate is the total of its two faces. A single plate with charge \(q\) given has \(\dfrac q2\) on each face.

Rules
  • The charge on the two outermost surfaces is always equal. For an isolated system its value is \(\dfrac{\text{total charge}}{2}\).
  • The charge on two facing surfaces is always equal and opposite (Gaussian surface through the material of both plates: \(0=q_{in}\), so \(q_1'=-q_2'\)).
  • Using conservation of charge for each plate, the total charge on each surface can be calculated.
  • For two plates carrying \(q_1\) and \(q_2\): outer faces each carry \(\dfrac{q_1+q_2}2\) and inner facing faces carry \(\pm\dfrac{q_1-q_2}2\).
  • The field between two facing surfaces carrying \(\pm q'\) is \(E=\dfrac{q'}{A\varepsilon_0}\), potential difference \(V=Ed=\dfrac{q'd}{A\varepsilon_0}\).
  • Earthing: the earthed plate's potential becomes zero; the free charge on its outer face becomes zero. Find the rest from conservation of charge and potential conditions (connected plates are at the same potential).
Derivation (two plates, field method)

Charge \(x\) on the left face of plate 1 and charges as in the figure. Zero field inside plate 1: \(E_1\) (from outer left) equals the sum of the others. For two plates: \(\dfrac{q_1-x}{2A\varepsilon_0}=\dfrac x{2A\varepsilon_0}+\dots\) gives \(x=\dfrac{q_1+q_2}2\); the right plate similarly, so the inner faces carry \(q_1-\dfrac{q_1+q_2}2=\dfrac{q_1-q_2}2\) and \(q_2-\dfrac{q_1+q_2}2=-\dfrac{q_1-q_2}2\).

Solved examples (plates)

Example 1 · Four plates with charges q, −2q, −3q, 8q

Four parallel plates carry \(q,-2q,-3q,8q\). Find the charge on each surface.

Let the left outer face carry \(x\). Conservation per plate gives the other faces (figure). The outermost faces are equal: \(4q-x=x\Rightarrow x=2q\).

Answer: plate 1: \(2q\) | \(-q\); plate 2: \(+q\) | \(-3q\); plate 3: \(+3q\) | \(-6q\); plate 4: \(+6q\) | \(+2q\).

Example 2 · Plates q and 2q, closing the earth switches S₁, S₂

Two plates carry \(q\) and \(2q\). Find the charge on each surface after closing (i) \(S_1\) (earths plate 1), (ii) \(S_2\) (earths plate 2), (iii) both.

Initial: outer faces \(\dfrac{3q}2\), inner faces \(-\dfrac q2,+\dfrac q2\).

(i) Plate 1 earthed: its outer face charge \(=0\); outer faces must be equal, so plate 2's outer face is also \(0\). Plate 2 (isolated, \(2q\)) must have \(2q\) on its inner face: facing faces \(-2q\) and \(+2q\).

(ii) Plate 2 earthed: outer faces \(0\); plate 1 (\(q\), isolated) puts \(q\) on its inner face; facing: \(+q\), \(-q\).

(iii) Both earthed: all faces \(0\) (no field, no charge).

Answers: (i) \(0,-2q,\ +2q,0\); (ii) \(0,+q,\ -q,0\); (iii) all zero.

Example 3 · Three plates, plates 1 and 3 connected

Plates 1, 2, 3 carry \(q\), \(2q\), \(5q\); plates 1 and 3 are connected by a wire (switch \(S\) closed). The gaps are \(a\) (1–2) and \(2a\) (2–3). Find the charge distribution.

The total charge is \(q+2q+5q=8q\), and the two outer faces are equal: \(2x=8q\Rightarrow x=4q\) each. Plate 2 is isolated and keeps \(2q\). Let plate 1 finally hold \(Q_1\) (plate 3 then holds \(6q-Q_1\)). The surface charges are: plate 1 right face \(Q_1-4q\); plate 2 left \(4q-Q_1\), right \(Q_1-2q\); plate 3 left \(2q-Q_1\).

Plates 1 and 3 are at the same potential, \(V_1=V_3\), so \(V_1-V_2=V_3-V_2\). In each gap \(E=\dfrac{\text{face charge}}{A\varepsilon_0}\), and \(V=Ed\) across the gap:

\[ (Q_1-4q)\,a=-(Q_1-2q)\,(2a)\;\Rightarrow\;3Q_1=8q\;\Rightarrow\;Q_1=\frac{8q}{3},\quad E_1=2E_2 \]

Answer: plate 1: \(4q\) | \(-\dfrac{4q}3\); plate 2: \(+\dfrac{4q}3\) | \(+\dfrac{2q}3\); plate 3: \(-\dfrac{2q}3\) | \(4q\).

Example 4 · Plates of 60 µC, 100 µC and 30 µC with earth switches

Three parallel plates X, Y, Z carry \(60\,\mu\text{C}\), \(100\,\mu\text{C}\) and \(30\,\mu\text{C}\). The gap X–Y is \(3d\) and the gap Y–Z is \(d\). Plate X can be earthed by switch \(S_1\) and plate Z by switch \(S_2\). Find the charge that flows to the ground (i) when only \(S_1\) is closed, (ii) when only \(S_2\) is closed, (iii) when both are closed.

For the earthed outermost plate the free outer-face charge becomes zero, so (isolated outer faces being equal) the total charge on the whole system becomes zero.

(i) Only \(S_1\) closed. Y and Z are isolated (100 and 30 µC). Total \(=0\Rightarrow X=-(100+30)=-130\,\mu\text{C}\). Faces: X: \(0\,|\,-130\); Y: \(+130\,|\,-30\); Z: \(+30\,|\,0\). Charge flowing to ground from X:

\[ 60-(-130)=190\ \mu\text{C} \]

(ii) Only \(S_2\) closed. Total \(=0\Rightarrow Z=-(60+100)=-160\,\mu\text{C}\). Faces: X: \(0\,|\,60\); Y: \(-60\,|\,160\); Z: \(-160\,|\,0\).

\[ 30-(-160)=190\ \mu\text{C} \]

(iii) Both closed. Y is isolated with \(100\,\mu\text{C}\). Let X's right face be \(a\): then Y's left face is \(-a\) and right face \(100+a\), and Z's left face is \(-(100+a)\); the outer faces are zero. X and Z are both at earth potential (\(V_X=V_Z\)):

\[ V_X-V_Y=\frac{a\,(3d)}{A\varepsilon_0},\quad V_Y-V_Z=\frac{(100+a)\,d}{A\varepsilon_0},\quad V_X=V_Z\Rightarrow3a=-(100+a)\Rightarrow a=-25 \]

So X \(=-25\,\mu\text{C}\), Y faces \(+25\,|\,+75\), Z \(=-75\,\mu\text{C}\). Charges flowing to ground:

\[ \text{from X: }60-(-25)=85\ \mu\text{C},\qquad \text{from Z: }30-(-75)=105\ \mu\text{C} \]

Answers: (i) \(190\,\mu\text{C}\) from X; (ii) \(190\,\mu\text{C}\) from Z; (iii) \(85\,\mu\text{C}\) from X and \(105\,\mu\text{C}\) from Z.

Example 5 · Outer-face charge of four plates

Four parallel plates carry \(q\), \(2q\), \(-4q\) and \(3q\) (isolated). Find the charge on each outer face.

Total charge \(=q+2q-4q+3q=2q\). The outer faces are equal, so \(2y=2q\).

Answer: \(y=q\) on each outer face; the inner faces follow plate by plate from conservation of charge (as in Example 1).

Conductor in a medium or in vacuum

  • In a medium: when a conductor is given charge beyond a certain limit, electrical breakdown of the surrounding medium takes place, due to which the charge of the conductor leaks to the surroundings.
  • In vacuum: on giving charge beyond a certain limit, i.e. when the electrostatic pressure exceeds the elastic strength of the material, the conductor explodes by its own state (it bursts because of its own charge).

Self Electrostatic Potential Energy

Self energy

The energy needed to assemble a charge distribution against its own repulsion. Standard values:

BodySelf energy
Uniformly charged disc (density \(\sigma\), radius \(R\))\(U_S=\dfrac{2\sigma^2R^3}{3\varepsilon_0}\)
Spherical shell\(U_S=\dfrac{KQ^2}{2R}\)
Uniform solid sphere\(U_S=\dfrac{3KQ^2}{5R}\)

Electrostatic potential energy density

Energy per unit volume is called energy density: \(u=\dfrac UV\) or \(u=\dfrac{dU}{dV}\), where \(dU\) is the energy associated with a small volume \(dV\).

Formula \[ u=\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2,\qquad U=\int\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2\,dV \]
Spherical shell

Outside, \(E=\dfrac{KQ}{r^2}\); use shells of volume \(dV=4\pi r^2dr\):

\[ U=\int_R^\infty\frac12\varepsilon_0\left(\frac{KQ}{r^2}\right)^24\pi r^2dr=\frac{KQ^2}2\int_R^\infty\frac{dr}{r^2}=\frac{KQ^2}{2R} \]
Solid sphere

Outside the field energy is \(\dfrac{KQ^2}{2R}\) (as for the shell). Inside, \(E=\dfrac{KQr}{R^3}\):

\[ U_{in}=\int_0^R\frac12\varepsilon_0\frac{K^2Q^2r^2}{R^6}\,4\pi r^2dr=\frac{KQ^2}{10R},\qquad U_{total}=\frac{KQ^2}{2R}+\frac{KQ^2}{10R}=\frac{3KQ^2}{5R} \]

Solved examples

Example 1 · Field energy in two regions around a shell

A spherical shell has radius \(R\) and charge \(Q\). Compare the electrostatic energy stored in the region \(R\) to \(2R\) (\(U_1\)) with that in the region \(2R\) to \(\infty\) (\(U_2\)).

\[ U_1=\frac{KQ^2}2\left[-\frac1r\right]_R^{2R}=\frac{KQ^2}2\left(\frac1R-\frac1{2R}\right)=\frac{KQ^2}{4R},\qquad U_2=\frac{KQ^2}2\left[-\frac1r\right]_{2R}^{\infty}=\frac{KQ^2}{4R} \]

Answer: \(U_1:U_2=1:1\) (each \(\dfrac{KQ^2}{4R}\)).

Example 2 · Self energy of a uniformly charged solid sphere

Find the electrostatic energy of a non-conducting solid sphere of radius \(R\) and uniform total charge \(Q\), by integrating over the field energy inside and outside.

Inside: \(\displaystyle\int_0^R\tfrac12\varepsilon_0\left(\tfrac{KQr}{R^3}\right)^24\pi r^2dr=\tfrac{KQ^2}{10R}\); outside: \(\tfrac{KQ^2}{2R}\).

Answer: \(U=\dfrac{KQ^2}{2R}+\dfrac{KQ^2}{10R}=\dfrac{3KQ^2}{5R}\).

Capacitance of a Conductor

Definition

The charge / energy storing capacity of a conductor is called its capacitance (or capacity).

Formula \[ C=\frac{\text{charge of conductor}}{\text{potential of conductor}}=\frac qV,\qquad q=CV\quad(q\propto V) \]
  • Capacitance is independent of the charge given to the conductor (\(q\)) and its potential (\(V\)); the ratio \(q/V\) stays fixed.
  • It depends on the shape and size of the conductor and on the nature of the surrounding medium.

Isolated spherical conductor, units and values

SI unit: farad (F). Practical units: \(\mu\text{F}\ (10^{-6})\), \(\text{nF}\ (10^{-9})\), \(\text{pF}\ (10^{-12})\).

Isolated spherical conductor of radius \(R\) \[ V=\frac q{4\pi\varepsilon_0R},\qquad C=\frac qV=4\pi\varepsilon_0R\qquad(C\propto R) \]
  • For the earth (\(R=6.4\times10^6\) m): \(C=\dfrac{R}{9\times10^9}=\dfrac{6.4\times10^6}{9\times10^9}\approx0.71\) mF (\(\approx7.1\times10^{-4}\) F).
  • For an earthed conductor \(V=0\), so \(C=\dfrac q0=\infty\).

Energy of a charged conductor

To charge a conductor from \(0\) to \(Q\), the work \(dW_{ext}=V\,dq\) is done in bringing a small charge \(dq\) when the conductor has charge \(q\) and potential \(V=\dfrac qC\):

\[ dW_{ext}=\frac{q\,dq}{C}\quad\Rightarrow\quad W_{ext}=\frac{Q^2}{2C} \]
Energy stored \[ U=\frac{Q^2}{2C}=\frac12CV^2=\frac12QV \]

Sharing of charge

Two conductors with capacitances \(C_1,C_2\), charges \(q_1=C_1V_1\), \(q_2=C_2V_2\), are joined by a wire (switch \(S\) closed). Charge flows until both reach a common potential \(V\), with new charges \(q_1'\), \(q_2'\).

From conservation of charge \[ q_1+q_2=q_1'+q_2'\ \Rightarrow\ C_1V_1+C_2V_2=(C_1+C_2)V \] \[ V=\frac{C_1V_1+C_2V_2}{C_1+C_2},\qquad \frac{q_1'}{q_2'}=\frac{C_1}{C_2} \] \[ q_1'=\frac{C_1(q_1+q_2)}{C_1+C_2},\qquad q_2'=\frac{C_2(q_1+q_2)}{C_1+C_2} \]
Loss of energy \[ U_{loss}=U_i-U_f=\tfrac12C_1V_1^2+\tfrac12C_2V_2^2-\tfrac12(C_1+C_2)V^2 \] \[ =\frac12\left[C_1V_1^2+C_2V_2^2-\frac{(C_1V_1+C_2V_2)^2}{C_1+C_2}\right]=\frac12\cdot\frac{C_1C_2V_1^2+C_1C_2V_2^2-2C_1C_2V_1V_2}{C_1+C_2} \]
Formula \[ U_{loss}=\frac12\,\frac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2}\,(V_1-V_2)^2 \]

Spherical conductors of radii \(r_1,r_2\) (\(C_1=4\pi\varepsilon_0r_1\), \(C_2=4\pi\varepsilon_0r_2\)):

Formulas \[ V=\frac{r_1V_1+r_2V_2}{r_1+r_2},\qquad \frac{q_1'}{q_2'}=\frac{r_1}{r_2} \] \[ q_1'=\frac{r_1(q_1+q_2)}{r_1+r_2},\quad q_2'=\frac{r_2(q_1+q_2)}{r_1+r_2},\qquad U_{loss}=\frac12\,\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0r_1r_2}{r_1+r_2}(V_1-V_2)^2 \]

Solved examples

Example 1 · Conductor repeatedly connected to an uncharged conducting sheet

A conductor A is kept at charge \(Q\) and connected, through a switch \(S\), to an uncharged conducting sheet B. When \(S\) is closed for the first time, a charge \(Q_0\) flows to B. \(S\) is then opened and the charge on A is again made \(Q\); the process is repeated for a very long time. Find the maximum charge acquired by the sheet.

First closing: \(V_A=V_B\): \(\dfrac{Q-Q_0}{C_A}=\dfrac{Q_0}{C_B}\), so \(\dfrac{C_B}{C_A}=\dfrac{Q_0}{Q-Q_0}\).

After many repetitions the sheet's potential equals that of A when A holds \(Q\); the sheet can acquire no more charge. With maximum charge \(x\) on B:

\[ \frac Q{C_A}=\frac x{C_B}\;\Rightarrow\;x=Q\,\frac{C_B}{C_A}=\frac{QQ_0}{Q-Q_0} \]

Answer: \(x=\dfrac{QQ_0}{Q-Q_0}\).

Example 2 · Repeated contact between a small and a large sphere

A small sphere of radius \(r\) is kept at charge \(Q\) and repeatedly connected (switch \(S\)) to a large uncharged sphere of radius \(R\); each time, \(S\) is opened and the small sphere's charge restored to \(Q\). Find the charge \(Q_n\) on the large sphere after \(n\) contacts, and its limiting value.

At the \(n\)th contact the total charge \(Q+Q_{n-1}\) is shared in proportion to the radii, the large sphere getting \(\dfrac R{r+R}\) of it. Let \(\alpha=\dfrac R{r+R}\):

\[ Q_1=\alpha Q,\quad Q_2=\alpha(Q+Q_1),\quad Q_n=\alpha(Q+Q_{n-1}) \] \[ Q_n=Q\left(\alpha+\alpha^2+\dots+\alpha^n\right)=Q\,\frac{\alpha\left(1-\alpha^n\right)}{1-\alpha}=\frac{QR}{r}\left[1-\left(\frac R{r+R}\right)^n\right] \]

Answer: \(Q_n=\dfrac{QR}{r}\left[1-\left(\dfrac R{R+r}\right)^n\right]\); as \(n\to\infty\), \(Q_n\to\dfrac{QR}{r}\).
